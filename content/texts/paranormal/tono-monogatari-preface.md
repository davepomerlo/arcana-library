::: note About this text
In 1908 Yanagita Kunio, then a young government official in Tokyo, met **Sasaki Kizen**, a student and aspiring writer from Tsuchibuchi village near Tōno. Over the following months Sasaki told him the stories of his home valley, and Yanagita wrote them down in a terse classical style and published 350 copies at his own expense. The book is now seen as the founding work of Japanese folklore studies (*minzokugaku* 民俗学).

The tales are short and matter-of-fact. Many name real families and villages, and some give dates. Among the beings they describe:

- **zashiki-warashi** 座敷童子: a child spirit living in old houses, whose departure brings the family's ruin;
- **kappa** 河童: water creatures, red-faced in Tōno, unlike the green kappa of other regions;
- **oshira-sama** オシラサマ: paired household deities made of mulberry wood, linked to a tale of a girl and a horse;
- **yamabito** 山人 and **yamaonna** 山女: the mountain men and women, whom Yanagita believed might be descendants of an older people driven into the mountains;
- **mayoiga** マヨイガ: a phantom house in the hills that gives wealth to those who take something from it.
:::

## The preface

::: parallel 1
此話はすべて遠野の人佐々木鏡石君より聞きたり。
昨明治四十二年の二月頃より始めて夜分折々訪ね来り此話をせられしを筆記せしなり。
鏡石君は話上手には非ざれども誠実なる人なり。
自分も亦一字一句をも加減せず感じたるまゝを書きたり。
---
Kono hanashi wa subete Tōno no hito Sasaki Kyōseki-kun yori kikitari.
Saku Meiji yonjūni-nen no nigatsu-goro yori hajimete yabun oriori tazune-kitari kono hanashi wo serareshi wo hikki seshi nari.
Kyōseki-kun wa hanashi-jōzu ni wa arazaredomo seijitsu naru hito nari.
Jibun mo mata ichiji ikku wo mo kagen sezu kanjitaru mama wo kakitari.
---
All these tales I heard from Mr Sasaki Kyōseki, a man of Tōno.
From about February of last year, Meiji 42 [1909], he came to visit me from time to time in the evenings and told them, and I wrote them down.
Kyōseki is not a gifted storyteller, but he is an honest man.
And I, for my part, have written them just as I felt them, without adding or taking away a single word.
:::

::: parallel 2
思ふに遠野郷には此類の物語猶数百件あるならん。
我々はより多くを聞かんことを切望す。
国内の山村にして遠野より更に物深き所には又無数の山神山人の伝説あるべし。
願はくは之を語りて平地人を戦慄せしめよ。
---
Omou ni Tōno-gō ni wa kono rui no monogatari nao sūhyakken aru naran.
Wareware wa yori ōku wo kikan koto wo setsubō su.
Kokunai no sanson ni shite Tōno yori sara ni mono-fukaki tokoro ni wa mata musū no yamagami yamabito no densetsu aru beshi.
Negawaku wa kore wo katarite heichijin wo senritsu seshimeyo.
---
I think there must still be several hundred more stories of this kind in the Tōno district.
We earnestly hope to hear more.
And in the mountain villages of this country that lie deeper still than Tōno, there must be countless legends of mountain gods and mountain people.
I pray that you will tell them, and make the people of the plains tremble.
:::

::: note "Make the people of the plains tremble"
The *heichijin* 平地人, "people of the flat lands", are the modern, rational, lowland Japanese of the cities. Yanagita wrote in the same preface that his book was "a record of present-day facts", and he meant its tales of spirits to unsettle readers who thought such things belonged to the past. The line has become a motto for Japanese writers on the uncanny ever since.
:::
