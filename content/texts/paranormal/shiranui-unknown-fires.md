## The founding legend

::: parallel Nihon shoki, Keikō 18.5
五月壬辰朔、從葦北發船到火國。於是日沒也、夜冥不知著岸。遙視火光、天皇詔挾杪者曰、直指火處。因指火往之、卽得著岸。天皇問其火光之處曰、何謂邑也。國人對曰、是八代縣豐村。亦尋其火、是誰人之火也、然不得主。茲知非人火。故名其國曰火國也。
---
In the fifth month, on the first day, the Emperor set sail from Ashikita 葦北 for the land of Hi. The sun went down, and in the darkness of night they could not tell where the shore was. Then they saw the light of fires far off. The Emperor ordered the helmsman: “Steer straight for the fires.” Steering by the fires, they reached the shore. The Emperor asked about the place where the fires had been: “What is this village called?” The people of the land answered: “It is Toyo village in the district of Yatsushiro 八代.” He asked further whose fires they had been, but no owner could be found. So it was known that they were not fires made by people. Therefore the land was named Hi no kuni 火國, “Land of Fire”.
:::

The word ***shiranui*** 不知火 means literally “fires nobody knows”, from this story of fires without an owner. *Shiranui no* (しらぬひの) became a fixed poetic epithet, *makurakotoba* 枕詞, for Tsukushi 筑紫, Kyushu, as early as the *Man'yōshū* 万葉集. The old province of Hi later split into Hizen 肥前 and Higo 肥後, and the *Hizen no kuni fudoki* 肥前国風土記 tells a related story.

## The phenomenon

Japanese dictionaries describe *shiranui* as countless lights flickering far out on the Yatsushiro Sea and the Ariake Sea at night. According to local tradition and modern observers, it is best seen:

- around **hassaku** 八朔, the first day of the eighth lunar month (late August to September), and also at the end of the twelfth month;
- on **moonless nights**, at low spring tide, roughly between 1 and 3:30 a.m.;
- from a raised viewpoint, traditionally the grounds of **Eino-o shrine** 永尾神社 in Uki 宇城市, which holds a *hassaku* festival on the night.

One or two “parent fires” (*oyabi* 親火) appear first. They then split to left and right until many lights stand in a long horizontal line, flickering and changing. Fishermen traditionally regarded the lights as uncanny and avoided going out on those nights. Kumamoto's local name for the sea, the Shiranui Sea 不知火海, comes from them.

## Explanation

Since the Meiji era, investigators have explained *shiranui* as an **abnormal refraction**, a kind of mirage. The light sources are distant fishing fires (*isaribi* 漁火), and layers of air of different temperature over the wide, shallow tidal flats split and stretch them. The flats are unusually long and straight because of the Hinagu fault. Around *hassaku*, the night air cools quickly while the sea is still warm, which favours the effect.

Since about 2017, a student team at Kumamoto Prefectural Uto High School has run regular observations from Eino-o shrine. They have photographed the light sources inverting and flickering, and have reproduced the stretching effect in the laboratory. They also note that the phenomenon has become hard to see. Reclamation of the tidal flats, electric lighting and the decline of torch fishing have taken away many of the conditions, and few local people today say they have seen it.

## Assessment

*Shiranui* is a rare case where a “mysterious light” of legend has a well-supported natural explanation, a written history going back almost 1,300 years, and an active modern research programme. Its interest now lies less in whether it is real (it is) than in how a mirage became the name of a province and a fixed image in Japanese poetry.
