## Nuremberg, 14 April 1561

A single-sheet woodcut with printed text, about 26 × 38 cm, was published in April 1561 by **Hans Glaser** (c. 1500–1573), a Nuremberg *Briefmaler* or “letter-painter”: a maker of cheap printed sheets. It reports that at dawn, between four and five in the morning, “many men and women” in the city and the countryside saw a terrifying sight on and around the sun.

In summary, the text describes:

- two blood-red half-moon arcs in the middle of the sun;
- globes of red, black and dull iron colour standing beside and around the sun, in rows and in squares;
- blood-red crosses and long rods or “tubes” among the globes, some containing further globes;
- the globes moving back and forth and “fighting” for more than an hour, before falling to earth “as if burning” and vanishing in smoke;
- a large black spear-like shape.

The sheet ends, like most such prints, with a call to repentance: the sight is read as a warning sign from God. The broadsheet survives in the **Wickiana**, the huge collection of news-sheets about prodigies assembled by the Zürich cleric Johann Jakob Wick (1522–1588), now in the Zentralbibliothek Zürich.

## Basel, 1566

A similar broadsheet, published by **Samuel Coccius** in Basel, reports that on 27–28 July and again on 7 August 1566, many large black globes and red-glowing spheres were seen at sunrise and sunset, moving quickly and seeming to fight one another.

## Context: prodigy sheets

Broadsheets (*Flugblätter*) reporting celestial wonders were a popular genre in sixteenth-century Germany and Switzerland. Glaser himself also printed sheets on other “miraculous signs”, among them knights fighting in the clouds and a blood rain in 1554. Their purpose was devotional and commercial as much as reportorial. Literacy was limited, and the pictures were often stock compositions. The skeptic Ulrich Magin has pointed out inconsistencies in the Nuremberg picture itself: for example, it shows a church that was not standing in that form at the time. Jacques Vallée, though himself a ufologist, regards this kind of material as sermon and propaganda rather than observation.

## Interpretations

**Atmospheric optics.** The most common natural explanation is a display of **sun dogs (parhelia)**, halos and arcs, produced when sunlight is refracted by ice crystals at sunrise. Sun dogs can look like extra suns on either side of the real one, and full halo displays can produce crosses, arcs and pillars. Critics note that the Nuremberg picture does not neatly match a classic halo display. But a woodcut made after the fact by a jobbing printer cannot be expected to be an accurate diagram.

**Symbolic reading.** C. G. Jung, who made the woodcut famous in his 1958 book on flying saucers, thought it most likely recorded a natural phenomenon onto which religious and military imagery had been projected.

**Ancient UFOs.** Since the 1960s, the woodcut has been widely reproduced as a “UFO battle”.

## Assessment

Both broadsheets are genuine sixteenth-century documents and almost certainly record *something* seen at sunrise by many people. What exactly was seen is beyond recovery. The pictures are interpretive, made in a genre designed to call people to repentance, and the descriptions fit, at least in part, known atmospheric phenomena.
