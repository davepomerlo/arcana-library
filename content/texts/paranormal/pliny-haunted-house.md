::: note Why this letter matters
This is the most influential ghost story of antiquity, and it already has every element of the modern haunted house: an empty, suspiciously cheap property, rattling chains, a spectre that beckons, and unquiet dead who rest once they are given proper burial. Pliny presents it not as entertainment but as evidence in a philosophical question. He asks his correspondent to weigh it, and to come down on one side.
:::

## The question

::: parallel 1
Et mihi discendi et tibi docendi facultatem otium praebet. Igitur perquam velim scire, esse phantasmata et habere propriam figuram numenque aliquod putes an inania et vana ex metu nostro imaginem accipere.
---
Our leisure gives me the chance to learn and you the chance to teach. So I would very much like to know whether you think ghosts exist, and have a form of their own and some kind of divine power, or whether they are empty and unreal and take their shape from our own fear.
:::

## First case: Curtius Rufus

::: parallel 2–3
Ego ut esse credam in primis eo ducor, quod audio accidisse Curtio Rufo. Tenuis adhuc et obscurus, obtinenti Africam comes haeserat. Inclinato die spatiabatur in porticu; offertur ei mulieris figura humana grandior pulchriorque. Perterrito Africam se futurorum praenuntiam dixit: iturum enim Romam honoresque gesturum, atque etiam cum summo imperio in eandem provinciam reversurum, ibique moriturum.
---
What chiefly leads me to believe they exist is what I hear happened to Curtius Rufus. While he was still poor and unknown, he had attached himself to the staff of the governor of Africa. Towards evening he was walking in a colonnade when the figure of a woman appeared to him, larger and more beautiful than any human. As he stood terrified, she said that she was Africa, and that she foretold the future: he would go to Rome and hold office, and would even return to this same province with supreme command, and die there.
:::

::: parallel 4
Facta sunt omnia. Praeterea accedenti Carthaginem egredientique nave eadem figura in litore occurrisse narratur. Ipse certe implicitus morbo futura praeteritis, adversa secundis auguratus, spem salutis nullo suorum desperante proiecit.
---
All of it came true. It is said, moreover, that as he approached Carthage and was stepping off the ship, the same figure met him on the shore. Certainly, when he fell ill, he read the future from the past and misfortune from good fortune, and gave up hope of recovery although none of his household despaired of him.
:::

## Second case: the house at Athens

::: parallel 5–6
Iam illud nonne et magis terribile et non minus mirum est quod exponam ut accepi? Erat Athenis spatiosa et capax domus sed infamis et pestilens. Per silentium noctis sonus ferri, et si attenderes acrius, strepitus vinculorum longius primo, deinde e proximo reddebatur: mox apparebat idolon, senex macie et squalore confectus, promissa barba horrenti capillo; cruribus compedes, manibus catenas gerebat quatiebatque.
---
Now is not this next story, which I will tell as I heard it, even more frightening and no less wonderful? There was at Athens a large and spacious house, but it had a bad name and was unhealthy. In the silence of the night came the sound of iron and, if you listened more closely, the clanking of chains, first far off and then very near. Then a phantom appeared: an old man worn out with emaciation and filth, with a long beard and bristling hair. He wore fetters on his legs and chains on his wrists, and he shook them.
:::

::: parallel 7
Inde inhabitantibus tristes diraeque noctes per metum vigilabantur; vigiliam morbus et crescente formidine mors sequebatur. Nam interdiu quoque, quamquam abscesserat imago, memoria imaginis oculis inerrabat, longiorque causis timoris timor erat. Deserta inde et damnata solitudine domus totaque illi monstro relicta; proscribebatur tamen, seu quis emere seu quis conducere ignarus tanti mali vellet.
---
So the people living there spent grim and dreadful nights awake with fear. Sleeplessness brought illness, and as their terror grew, death followed. For even by day, when the apparition had gone, the memory of it hovered before their eyes, and the fear outlasted its causes. In the end the house was deserted, condemned to emptiness and left entirely to the monster. Still, it was advertised, in case anyone who knew nothing of so great an evil wanted to buy or rent it.
:::

::: parallel 8–9
Venit Athenas philosophus Athenodorus, legit titulum auditoque pretio, quia suspecta vilitas, percunctatus omnia docetur ac nihilo minus, immo tanto magis conducit. Ubi coepit advesperascere, iubet sterni sibi in prima domus parte, poscit pugillares stilum lumen, suos omnes in interiora dimittit; ipse ad scribendum animum oculos manum intendit, ne vacua mens audita simulacra et inanes sibi metus fingeret.
---
The philosopher Athenodorus came to Athens and read the notice. When he heard the price, he was suspicious of how cheap it was. He made enquiries and was told everything, and he rented the house all the same, indeed all the more eagerly. As evening drew on, he had a couch made up for himself in the front part of the house and called for writing tablets, a stylus and a lamp. He sent all his household to the inner rooms and bent his mind, eyes and hand to writing, so that an idle mind would not invent the phantoms he had heard of and empty fears.
:::

::: parallel 10
Initio, quale ubique, silentium noctis; dein concuti ferrum, vincula moveri. Ille non tollere oculos, non remittere stilum, sed offirmare animum auribusque praetendere. Tum crebrescere fragor, adventare et iam ut in limine, iam ut intra limen audiri. Respicit, videt agnoscitque narratam sibi effigiem.
---
At first there was the silence of night, as everywhere. Then iron began to clank and chains to move. He did not raise his eyes or put down his stylus, but steeled his mind and kept it in front of his ears. Then the din grew louder and came closer, and now it could be heard at the threshold, now inside it. He looked round and saw, and recognised, the figure that had been described to him.
:::

::: parallel 11
Stabat innuebatque digito similis vocanti. Hic contra ut paulum exspectaret manu significat rursusque ceris et stilo incumbit. Illa scribentis capiti catenis insonabat. Respicit rursus idem quod prius innuentem, nec moratus tollit lumen et sequitur.
---
It stood there and beckoned with its finger, as if calling him. He in turn signalled with his hand that it should wait a little, and bent again over his wax tablets and stylus. It rattled its chains over the head of the writing man. He looked round again and saw it beckoning as before. Without delay he picked up the lamp and followed.
:::

::: parallel 12–13
Ibat illa lento gradu quasi gravis vinculis. Postquam deflexit in aream domus, repente dilapsa deserit comitem. Desertus herbas et folia concerpta signum loco ponit. Postero die adit magistratus, monet ut illum locum effodi iubeant. Inveniuntur ossa inserta catenis et implicita, quae corpus aevo terraque putrefactum nuda et exesa reliquerat vinculis; collecta publice sepeliuntur. Domus postea rite conditis manibus caruit.
---
It went with a slow step, as if weighed down by its chains. After it turned into the courtyard of the house, it suddenly vanished and left its companion. Left alone, he plucked some grass and leaves and put them on the spot as a marker. The next day he went to the magistrates and urged them to have the place dug up. Bones were found, bound and entangled in chains: the body, rotted by time and the earth, had left them bare and eaten away in their fetters. They were gathered up and buried at public expense. After that, once the spirit had been duly laid to rest, the house was free of it.
:::

## Third case: in Pliny's own house

::: parallel 14
Et haec quidem adfirmantibus credo; illud adfirmare aliis possum. Est libertus mihi non illitteratus. Cum hoc minor frater eodem lecto quiescebat. Is visus est sibi cernere quendam in toro residentem, admoventemque capiti suo cultros, atque etiam ex ipso vertice amputantem capillos. Ubi illuxit, ipse circa verticem tonsus, capilli iacentes reperiuntur.
---
These things I believe on the word of those who vouch for them. The next I can vouch for to others myself. I have a freedman who is not without education. His younger brother was sleeping in the same bed with him. The brother dreamed he saw someone sitting on the couch, bringing a pair of scissors to his head and cutting the hair from the very crown. When day broke, he was found shorn around the crown, and the hair was lying there.
:::

::: parallel 15
Exiguum temporis medium, et rursus simile aliud priori fidem fecit. Puer in paedagogio mixtus pluribus dormiebat. Venerunt per fenestras — ita narrat — in tunicis albis duo cubantemque detonderunt et qua venerant recesserunt. Hunc quoque tonsum sparsosque circa capillos dies ostendit.
---
A short time passed, and another case like it confirmed the first. A slave boy was sleeping among several others in the pages' quarters. Two figures in white tunics came in through the windows (so he says), cut his hair as he lay there, and went back the way they had come. Daylight showed that he too had been shorn, with the hair scattered round him.
:::

::: parallel 16
Nihil notabile secutum, nisi forte quod non fui reus, futurus, si Domitianus sub quo haec acciderunt diutius vixisset. Nam in scrinio eius datus a Caro de me libellus inventus est; ex quo coniectari potest, quia reis moris est summittere capillum, recisos meorum capillos depulsi quod imminebat periculi signum fuisse.
---
Nothing remarkable followed, unless perhaps that I was not put on trial, as I would have been if Domitian, under whom this happened, had lived longer. For in his desk was found an accusation against me lodged by Carus. From this one may guess that, since it is the custom of the accused to let their hair grow, the cutting of my slaves' hair was a sign that the danger hanging over me had been averted.
:::

## The request

::: parallel 17–18
Proinde rogo, eruditionem tuam intendas. Digna res est quam diu multumque consideres; ne ego quidem indignus, cui copiam scientiae tuae facias. Licet etiam utramque in partem — ut soles — disputes, ex altera tamen fortius, ne me suspensum incertumque dimittas, cum mihi consulendi causa fuerit, ut dubitare desinerem. Vale.
---
So I ask you to bring your learning to bear. The matter deserves long and careful thought, and I am not unworthy to share your knowledge. You may argue both sides, as you usually do, but argue one side more strongly, so as not to leave me hanging in doubt. My reason for consulting you was to stop doubting. Farewell.
:::

::: note Afterword
Sura's answer, if he sent one, does not survive. Similar stories of a haunted house were told by Lucian (*Philopseudes* 30–31) and in Plautus' comedy *Mostellaria*, “The Haunted House”. This suggests that Pliny's tale was a well-known type even in antiquity, whatever one makes of its truth.
:::
