## What the sources say

The story appears in several Edo-period texts that differ in their details. In outline, they report that early in 1803, fishermen on the coast of Hitachi Province saw a strange vessel drifting offshore and towed it in. It was round, often compared to an incense burner (*kōro* 香炉) or a rice-cooking pot, with a thickened rim around its middle and windows of glass or crystal. Inside were unfamiliar writing, food and water. A young woman of about eighteen to twenty, with unusual clothes and hair (some versions say red), sat inside holding a small box that she would not let anyone touch. She could not make herself understood. In most versions, the villagers pushed her and her vessel back out to sea.

The best-known version is in the ***Toen shōsetsu*** 兎園小説 (1825). This is a collection of strange tales and rumours compiled by the Toenkai 兎園会 circle and edited by the novelist Kyokutei Bakin 曲亭馬琴, author of *Nansō Satomi Hakkenden*. Its illustration of a saucer-like ship and a woman with a box is the one usually reproduced today.

Other early accounts include:

| Text | Date | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| *Ōshuku zakki* 鶯宿雑記 | 1815 | Brief early mention |
| *Toen shōsetsu* 兎園小説 | 1825 | Kyokutei Bakin's circle; gives the date as 22 February 1803 and the place as Harayadori はらやどり |
| *Hyōryū kishū* 漂流記集 | 1835 | Collection of castaway tales |
| *Ume no chiri* 梅の塵 | 1844 | Nagahashi Matajirō; gives a different date (24 March 1803) and place (Haratonohama 原舎浜) |

The researcher Tanaka Kazuo 田中嘉津夫, professor emeritus at Gifu University, has located eleven documents relating to the Hitachi story. The most interesting of them appear to date from 1803 itself, the year of the supposed landing.

## Where the sources disagree

- **Date and place.** The texts give different months and different beach names. Tanaka has noted that the place name “Harayadori” does not appear to correspond to any known location.
- **The pictures.** The illustrations vary a good deal. Even the Edo writers remarked that the drawings did not match the descriptions closely.
- **What it was.** Not one of the accounts describes the vessel flying or moving under its own power. It drifts.

## Interpretations

**Folklore.** The folklorist Yanagita Kunio 柳田國男 treated *utsuro-bune* stories as part of an old Japanese pattern of tales in which a sacred or exiled person arrives from the sea in a closed vessel. Similar “hollow boat” stories are found elsewhere in Japan.

**Rumour and promotion.** Tanaka has suggested that the story spread as a rumour about a beach at Kashimanada 鹿島灘, and that people at a local temple, Shōfukuji, may then have taken it up to promote the temple.

**A foreign woman.** Some writers have suggested a shipwrecked or exiled foreigner, perhaps a European or Russian woman, arriving at a time when Japan was closed to outsiders.

**UFO.** From the twentieth century onward, ufologists have compared the saucer shape and the unknown writing with modern “close encounter” reports. Tanaka himself thinks these comparisons are far-fetched, chiefly because the vessel never flies.

## Assessment

There is good evidence that a story about a hollow ship and a woman with a box circulated widely in Edo Japan around 1803, and that serious writers recorded it. There is no physical evidence, the details are inconsistent, and the story fits a known folk pattern. What survives is a well-documented *rumour*, which is itself historically interesting: a window onto how a closed society imagined the outside world.
