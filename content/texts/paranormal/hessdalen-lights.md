## The lights

Hessdalen is a valley about 12 km long in central Norway, with only around a hundred residents. Lights have been reported there since at least the 1930s. Between late 1981 and 1984 there were so many reports that the valley drew national attention, with sightings at times reported several times a week. By about 2010 the rate had fallen to some 10–20 a year.

The researchers who have studied them describe three main types:

1. **White or blue flashes** lasting a few seconds at most;
2. **Large yellow or white lights**, often stationary or drifting slowly, sometimes for an hour or more, and sometimes moving at very high speed;
3. **Clusters** of several lights moving together.

They have been seen by day as well as by night, and recorded on photographs, video and radar. No heat radiation has ever been measured from them, and there are no burn marks.

## The research

- **1983–1985 — Project Hessdalen.** Founded on 3 June 1983 by a small group from the Norwegian and Swedish UFO societies, with scientific advisers from the universities of Oslo and Bergen. An instrumented field campaign ran from 21 January to 26 February 1984, with radar, a seismograph, magnetometers, a spectrum analyser and cameras, followed by a shorter campaign in January 1985.
- **1998 — the automatic station.** Østfold University College set up the **Hessdalen Automatic Measurement Station**, the “Blue Box”, which records the valley continuously with cameras and other instruments.
- **2000 onward — EMBLA.** A collaboration with Italian scientists, including the Institute of Radio Astronomy (IRA-INAF) in Bologna, brought in spectroscopy and radio instruments. Annual **Science Camps** for school students have been held in the valley since 2002. French groups (CNRS, CEA) later set up temporary electromagnetic stations.

According to Strand and colleagues, the work established that the phenomenon is measurable. Light events have coincided with magnetic disturbances and strong but intermittent radar returns, and observations were more frequent during periods of high geomagnetic activity. Spectra suggest a hot, glowing gas or plasma.

## Hypotheses

There is still **no agreed explanation**. Proposals include:

- **dusty plasma** formed from particles lifted from the valley floor;
- **piezoelectricity** from stressed quartz-bearing rock, although the area has very low seismic activity;
- a **natural “battery”** effect involving minerals in the valley rocks and the river that runs through it;
- **combustion or ionisation** of gases released from the ground;
- ordinary misidentifications (aircraft, car headlights, astronomical objects), which account for some reports but not, the researchers argue, for the instrumented cases.

## Assessment

Hessdalen shows what a serious study of an anomalous phenomenon can look like: patient, instrumented, published and open about its uncertainties. Whatever the lights turn out to be, the real phenomenon is not in doubt. Its physics remains an open research question.
