## The two sources

The story survives in two accounts written independently, within a generation or so of each other:

- **William of Newburgh**, an Augustinian canon in Yorkshire, wrote his *History of English Affairs* in 1195–98. He devotes Book 1, chapter 27 to the green children. He admits that he hesitated to believe the story, but was overwhelmed by the number and quality of the witnesses.
- **Ralph of Coggeshall**, later abbot of a Cistercian house in Essex about 25 miles from Woolpit, gives a fuller version in his *English Chronicle*. He says he heard it repeatedly from **Sir Richard de Calne** of Wikes, the knight in whose household the girl lived as a servant.

There are very few word-for-word overlaps between the two. Most scholars, Clark included, think both writers drew independently on a common story that was circulating locally.

## What they report

In summary, at harvest time two children, a boy and a girl, were found by the “wolf pits” (Old English *wulf-pytt*) that gave Woolpit its name. Their skin was green all over and their clothing was strange. They spoke a language no one understood. They would eat nothing until they were offered raw green beans, which they ate eagerly. The boy grew sickly and soon died. The girl adapted to ordinary food and gradually lost her green colour. She was baptised, learned English and lived for many years. William says she married at King's Lynn; Ralph describes her as rather wanton and impudent.

Asked where they came from, she said they were from a land where everyone and everything was green, which had no sun but a perpetual twilight, like the time just after sunset. A bright land could be seen across a great river. William has her call it **St Martin's Land**. They had been tending their father's flocks and followed a sound of bells, or wandered into a cavern, and came out into the dazzling light at Woolpit.

## Interpretations

- **Fairy or otherworld tale.** The details fit well-known medieval motifs: an underground twilight land, fairy food and a passage through a cave. Many folklorists read the story as a local legend told in chronicle form.
- **Lost Flemish children.** In the 1990s, Paul Harris argued that the children were orphans of Flemish immigrants persecuted under Henry II. On this view they wandered from the village of Fornham St Martin, which would explain “St Martin's Land”, and their green colour came from malnutrition (chlorosis). Critics note that this depends on redating the event and on taking the children's account literally.
- **Science fiction readings.** The children have also been claimed as visitors from another world or a parallel dimension.

## Assessment

This is unusually well sourced for a medieval wonder: two independent chroniclers within living memory, one of them naming the family who knew the girl. But both wrote in a genre of *mirabilia* (wonders), and the core of the story is the girl's own account, filtered through the people who retold it. Clark's 2024 study, which translates both Latin texts and sets them side by side, is now the place to start.
