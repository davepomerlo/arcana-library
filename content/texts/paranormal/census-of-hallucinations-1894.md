## Background

The **Society for Psychical Research** (SPR) was founded in London in 1882 by a group of Cambridge scholars, including the philosopher Henry Sidgwick, to investigate claims of telepathy, apparitions and mediumship by scientific methods. In 1886, Edmund Gurney, F. W. H. Myers and Frank Podmore published ***Phantasms of the Living***, two thick volumes of carefully documented cases of “crisis apparitions”. These are cases in which someone sees or hears a person at about the moment that person is dying or in danger far away. Critics objected that such coincidences would happen by chance among millions of people.

## Method

To answer this, an SPR committee chaired by **Henry Sidgwick** set out to estimate how often ordinary, sane people have waking hallucinations at all. The other members were **Eleanor Mildred (Nora) Sidgwick**, Alice Johnson, F. W. H. Myers, A. T. Myers and Frank Podmore. From 1889, volunteer collectors put a single question to people they knew. The question asked whether, when fully awake, the person had ever had a vivid impression of seeing or being touched by a living being or inanimate object, or of hearing a voice, which impression, as far as they could discover, was not due to any external physical cause. Dreams were excluded.

**Some 17,000 replies** were collected, and **1,684** people answered “yes”. Each positive answer was followed up with questions about the details, dates and any corroboration. The International Congress of Experimental Psychology encouraged similar surveys in France, Germany and the United States.

## Results

The final report, drawn up mainly by Nora Sidgwick, ran to nearly 400 pages in volume 10 of the SPR's *Proceedings* (August 1894). Its chapters cover the trustworthiness of the answers, the relation between illusions and hallucinations, health, age and sex, expectation and suggestion, and, at the heart of the report, **death-coincidences**.

The committee estimated how many recognised apparitions of living people would be expected to fall, by pure chance, within twelve hours of that person's death. It then compared this with the number actually reported, after discounting for forgetting and exaggeration. Its conclusion was:

> Between deaths and apparitions of the dying person a connexion exists which is not due to chance alone. This we hold to be a proved fact.

## Criticism

Contemporaries, including the German psychologist **Edmund Parish**, argued that memory distorts in a systematic way. A vivid experience that happens to coincide with a death is remembered and retold, while non-coincidental experiences are forgotten. Details are also unconsciously “read back” after the news arrives. The committee had tried to correct for exactly this, but critics doubted that any correction could be adequate. Later statisticians have also questioned the assumptions behind the baseline estimate.

## Why it still matters

Whatever one concludes about crisis apparitions, the Census was a pioneering piece of survey research on hallucinations in the general population. It showed, for the first time, that such experiences were common among healthy people: roughly one in ten reported at least one. Modern research on “hallucinations in the non-clinical population” and on grief-related apparitions often goes back to it.
