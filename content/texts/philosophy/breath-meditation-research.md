::: note How to read this guide
Contemplative traditions make claims about the mind that can partly be tested. This guide summarises six influential studies, chosen because they are well designed or because they are much cited, and says what each actually found. It is not medical advice. The archive's primary texts on these practices are the [Ānāpānasati Sutta](anapanasati-breath.html), the [Yoga Sūtras](yoga-sutras-selections.html) and the [Haṭhapradīpikā](hatha-pradipika-pranayama.html).

## Meditation

**Goyal et al. 2014, Johns Hopkins.** A systematic review and meta-analysis of 47 randomised trials (3,515 participants) that compared meditation programmes with *active* controls, not just with doing nothing. It found:

- moderate evidence that mindfulness programmes reduce anxiety, depression and pain, with small to moderate effects;
- low evidence for reduced stress and better mental-health-related quality of life;
- little or no evidence of an effect on positive mood, attention, sleep, eating habits or weight;
- no evidence that meditation works better than other active treatments such as drugs, exercise or behavioural therapy.

**Van Dam et al. 2018, Melbourne and 14 co-authors.** A critical review by leading meditation researchers. Its message is that public enthusiasm has outrun the evidence: "mindfulness" is used for many different things, many studies lack proper control groups, adverse effects are rarely measured, and brain-imaging results are often overstated. The authors do not dismiss meditation; they set out how research must improve.

## Slow breathing

**Zaccaro et al. 2018, University of Pisa.** A systematic review of breathing slower than ten breaths a minute, including *prāṇāyāma*. Of 2,461 abstracts, only 15 studies met its criteria. In those studies, slow breathing:

- increased heart-rate variability and respiratory sinus arrhythmia, signs of parasympathetic ("rest and digest") activity;
- increased alpha and decreased theta power in the EEG;
- was linked with feeling more relaxed, comfortable and alert, and less anxious, depressed or angry.

The authors propose two mechanisms: conscious attention to the body's internal states, and the effect of slow airflow on receptors in the nose that influence brain rhythms. Both remain hypotheses.

**Yilmaz Balban et al. 2023, Stanford.** A one-month randomised trial with 108 participants, comparing five minutes a day of mindfulness meditation with three breathing exercises: cyclic sighing (a double inhale and a long exhale), box breathing and cyclic hyperventilation with retention. All four improved mood and reduced anxiety. Cyclic sighing improved positive mood and lowered resting breathing rate more than meditation did. There were no significant changes in heart-rate variability or sleep. The study was run remotely, mostly with university students.

## Forceful breathing and cold

**Kox et al. 2014, Radboud University Medical Center.** Twelve healthy young men trained for ten days by Wim Hof in breathing exercises, cold exposure and meditation were injected with a bacterial endotoxin, together with twelve untrained controls. During the breathing exercises the trained men produced large amounts of adrenaline, and they had fewer flu-like symptoms and a weaker inflammatory response. The study showed for the first time that this part of the immune response can be influenced voluntarily. It did not test long-term health effects. Hyperventilation and breath-holding can cause fainting, and have caused deaths when practised in water.

## Mystical experience

**Griffiths et al. 2006, Johns Hopkins.** In a double-blind trial, 36 volunteers with no previous experience of hallucinogens received psilocybin in one session and an active placebo (methylphenidate) in another, with careful preparation and support. Many described experiences that met the criteria of standard mysticism questionnaires, and two months later most rated them among the most meaningful experiences of their lives, reporting positive changes that friends and family also noticed. Some volunteers experienced strong fear or anxiety during the session. The study revived scientific research on psychedelics and on mystical experience itself.

## What remains open

- Most trials are short, and few follow participants for more than a year.
- Practices are hard to blind: participants usually know which group they are in.
- Studies are often run by teachers or advocates of the method being tested.
- Traditional texts aim at liberation or insight, which trials do not try to measure.
