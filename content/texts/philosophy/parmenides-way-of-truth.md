::: note Context
Parmenides wrote a single poem, *On Nature*, in the metre of Homer. The surviving prologue (fragment 1) describes a chariot journey through the gates of Night and Day to an unnamed goddess. What she tells him falls into two parts: the “Way of Truth”, given here, and the “Way of Opinion” about the world of the senses, which survives only in scraps.
:::

::: parallel B2
εἰ δ᾽ ἄγ᾽ ἐγὼν ἐρέω, κόμισαι δὲ σὺ μῦθον ἀκούσας,
αἵπερ ὁδοὶ μοῦναι διζήσιός εἰσι νοῆσαι·
ἡ μὲν ὅπως ἔστιν τε καὶ ὡς οὐκ ἔστι μὴ εἶναι,
Πειθοῦς ἐστι κέλευθος (Ἀληθείῃ γὰρ ὀπηδεῖ),
ἡ δ᾽ ὡς οὐκ ἔστιν τε καὶ ὡς χρεών ἐστι μὴ εἶναι,
τὴν δή τοι φράζω παναπευθέα ἔμμεν ἀταρπόν·
οὔτε γὰρ ἂν γνοίης τό γε μὴ ἐὸν (οὐ γὰρ ἀνυστόν)
οὔτε φράσαις.
---
Come now, I will tell you, and you, when you have heard, carry the account away:
which are the only roads of inquiry there are to think of.
The one, that it is and that it cannot not be,
is the path of Persuasion, for she attends on Truth;
the other, that it is not and that it must not be,
this, I tell you, is a path wholly beyond learning.
For you could not know what is not (that cannot be done),
nor could you point it out.
:::

::: parallel B3
…τὸ γὰρ αὐτὸ νοεῖν ἐστίν τε καὶ εἶναι.
---
…for the same thing is for thinking and for being.
(Or: for thinking and being are the same.)
:::

::: parallel B6
χρὴ τὸ λέγειν τε νοεῖν τ᾽ ἐὸν ἔμμεναι· ἔστι γὰρ εἶναι,
μηδὲν δ᾽ οὐκ ἔστιν· τά σ᾽ ἐγὼ φράζεσθαι ἄνωγα.
---
What is there to be said and thought must be; for it is there to be,
but nothing is not. These things I bid you ponder.
:::

::: parallel B8
μοῦνος δ᾽ ἔτι μῦθος ὁδοῖο
λείπεται ὡς ἔστιν· ταύτῃ δ᾽ ἐπὶ σήματ᾽ ἔασι
πολλὰ μάλ᾽, ὡς ἀγένητον ἐὸν καὶ ἀνώλεθρόν ἐστιν,
οὖλον μουνογενές τε καὶ ἀτρεμὲς ἠδ᾽ ἀτέλεστον·
οὐδέ ποτ᾽ ἦν οὐδ᾽ ἔσται, ἐπεὶ νῦν ἔστιν ὁμοῦ πᾶν,
ἕν, συνεχές·
---
One account of a road is still left:
that it is. On this road there are very many signs:
that, being, it is unborn and imperishable,
whole, of a single kind, unshaken and complete.
Nor was it ever, nor will it be, since it is now, all together,
one, continuous.
:::

::: note Translation notes
**ἔστιν *estin*** (“is”) has no stated subject, and this is deliberate. Scholars argue over whether to supply “it”, “what is”, “the object of inquiry” or nothing at all. The translation uses “it is” and leaves the question open.

**Fragment 8** goes on for another fifty-five lines. They argue that what is cannot come to be, change, move or be divided, and end with the image of a well-rounded sphere.
:::
