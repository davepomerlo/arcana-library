::: note About this text
Plotinus, probably born in Lycopolis in Upper Egypt, studied in Alexandria for eleven years with the philosopher Ammonius Saccas and then taught in Rome from 244 until shortly before his death in 270. He wrote nothing until he was about fifty. His pupil Porphyry arranged his 54 treatises into six groups of nine, the *Enneads* ("nines").

At the centre of his thought is the One, or the Good: beyond being and beyond thought, the source from which Intellect (*Nous*) and Soul flow out "like light from the sun", without the One losing anything. The human soul belongs to that higher world and can return to it, not by ritual, but by inward purification and contemplation.

*On Beauty* (I.6), by Porphyry's count the first treatise Plotinus wrote, became his most influential: Renaissance artists and thinkers from Ficino onward read it as a theory of art and love. Its climax is the image of the soul as a statue that each person must carve.
:::

## Sculpt your own statue (I.6.9)

::: parallel I.6.9
Πῶς ἂν οὖν ἴδοις ψυχὴν ἀγαθὴν οἷον τὸ κάλλος ἔχει; Ἄναγε ἐπὶ σαυτὸν καὶ ἴδε· κἂν μήπω σαυτὸν ἴδῃς καλόν, οἷα ποιητὴς ἀγάλματος, ὃ δεῖ καλὸν γενέσθαι, τὸ μὲν ἀφαιρεῖ, τὸ δὲ ἀπέξεσε, τὸ δὲ λεῖον, τὸ δὲ καθαρὸν ἐποίησεν, ἕως ἔδειξε καλὸν ἐπὶ τῷ ἀγάλματι πρόσωπον, οὕτω καὶ σὺ ἀφαίρει ὅσα περιττὰ καὶ ἀπεύθυνε ὅσα σκολιά, ὅσα σκοτεινὰ καθαίρων ἐργάζου εἶναι λαμπρὰ καὶ μὴ παύσῃ τεκταίνων τὸ σὸν ἄγαλμα, ἕως ἂν ἐκλάμψειέ σοι τῆς ἀρετῆς ἡ θεοειδὴς ἀγλαία, ἕως ἂν ἴδῃς σωφροσύνην ἐν ἁγνῷ βεβῶσαν βάθρῳ.
---
How, then, can you see what kind of beauty a good soul has? Go back into yourself and look. And if you do not yet see yourself as beautiful, do as the maker of a statue that is meant to be beautiful: he cuts away here, scrapes there, smooths this part and cleans that, until he has brought out a beautiful face on the statue. So you too: cut away everything superfluous, straighten what is crooked, clear what is dark and work to make it bright, and never stop carving your own statue, until the godlike splendour of virtue shines out on you, until you see self-mastery standing on its holy pedestal.
:::

## Becoming sunlike (I.6.9, end)

::: parallel I.6.9b
οὐ γὰρ πώποτε εἶδεν ὀφθαλμὸς ἥλιον ἡλιοειδὴς μὴ γεγενημένος, οὐδὲ τὸ καλὸν ἂν ἴδοι ψυχὴ μὴ καλὴ γενομένη.
---
For no eye ever saw the sun without first becoming sunlike, and no soul could see beauty without first becoming beautiful.
:::

## The last words of the Enneads (VI.9.11)

::: parallel VI.9.11
καὶ οὗτος θεῶν καὶ ἀνθρώπων θείων καὶ εὐδαιμόνων βίος, ἀπαλλαγὴ τῶν ἄλλων τῶν τῇδε, βίος ἀνήδονος τῶν τῇδε, φυγὴ μόνου πρὸς μόνον.
---
And this is the life of gods and of godlike and blessed humans: a release from all other things here, a life that takes no pleasure in the things of this world, a flight of the alone to the alone.
:::

::: note Reading Plotinus
- **The statue.** Plotinus borrows the image from Plato's *Phaedrus* (252d), where it is the *lover* who shapes the beloved's soul like a statue of the god. Plotinus turns it inward: everyone must work on their own. Some critics, most famously Julia Kristeva, have called this narcissistic. Daniel Tolan, in the Harvard Theological Review, argues the opposite: Plotinus spent his life working on his students' "statues", and his mysticism expanded rather than shrank his concern for others.
- **The sun.** The sun sentence develops Plato's *Republic* (508b), where the eye is called "the most sunlike of the sense organs". Goethe later made it famous in German: *Wär nicht das Auge sonnenhaft, die Sonne könnt es nie erblicken.* Some older editions read οὐ γὰρ ἂν πώποτε.
- **"Flight of the alone to the alone."** Porphyry placed this treatise last, so these became the closing words of the *Enneads*. *Monos* means "alone" but also "single, unified": the soul that has become one meets the One.
:::
