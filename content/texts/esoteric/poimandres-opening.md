::: note About this text
The *Corpus Hermeticum* is a collection of seventeen Greek treatises attributed to Hermes Trismegistus, "thrice-greatest Hermes", a Greek form of the Egyptian god Thoth. When Marsilio Ficino translated them into Latin in 1463, at Cosimo de' Medici's request and before finishing Plato, he believed they were older than Moses. In 1614 Isaac Casaubon showed from their language that they date from the Roman period.

The first treatise, *Poimandres*, is the most famous. It tells of a vision in which a divine Mind (*Nous*) reveals the creation of the world, the fall of the archetypal Human into matter, and the soul's ascent back through the planetary spheres. Its opening, given here, is one of the earliest first-person accounts of a mystical vision in Greek.

The name *Poimandres* is unexplained. It was once read as Greek "shepherd of men"; many scholars now prefer an Egyptian origin, perhaps "knowledge of Re" or "the mind of sovereignty", as the text itself glosses it.
:::

Once, when I had begun to think about the things that are, and my mind had soared very high while my bodily senses were held in check, like those weighed down by sleep after too much food or after toil of the body, it seemed to me that a being of immense size, beyond all measure, was calling my name and saying to me: "What do you wish to hear and to see, and, having understood, to learn and to know?"
:::

Having said this, he changed his form, and at once, in an instant, everything was opened to me, and I see a boundless vision: everything had become light, serene and joyful, and seeing it I fell in love with it.

## The meaning of the light

::: parallel 6
ὁ δὲ Ποιμάνδρης ἐμοί, Ἐνόησας, φησί, τὴν θέαν ταύτην ὅ τι καὶ βούλεται; καί, Γνώσομαι, ἔφην ἐγώ. Τὸ φῶς ἐκεῖνο, ἔφη, ἐγὼ Νοῦς ὁ σὸς θεός, ὁ πρὸ φύσεως ὑγρᾶς τῆς ἐκ σκότους φανείσης· ὁ δὲ ἐκ Νοὸς φωτεινὸς Λόγος υἱὸς θεοῦ. Τί οὖν; φημί. Οὕτω γνῶθι· τὸ ἐν σοὶ βλέπον καὶ ἀκοῦον, λόγος κυρίου, ὁ δὲ νοῦς πατὴρ θεός. οὐ γὰρ διίστανται ἀπ' ἀλλήλων· ἕνωσις γὰρ τούτων ἐστὶν ἡ ζωή. Εὐχαριστῶ σοι, ἔφην ἐγώ.
---
Then Poimandres said to me: "Have you understood what this vision means?" "I shall know," I said. "That light," he said, "is I, Mind, your God, who was before the watery nature that appeared out of the darkness. And the luminous Word that comes from Mind is the son of God." "What then?" I said. "Know it in this way: what sees and hears in you is the word of the Lord, and mind is God the Father. They are not divided from one another, for their union is life." "I thank you," I said.
:::

::: note Reading the Poimandres
Section 5, left out here, describes the second half of the vision: a dark, coiling, watery nature that cries out, and a holy Word that comes down upon it. Scholars have long debated how much the treatise owes to Genesis and to Jewish thought in Alexandria, and how much to Egyptian religion. The phrase "luminous Word, son of God" invites comparison with the prologue of John's gospel, but the two texts are probably independent witnesses to shared ideas rather than one borrowing from the other.
:::
