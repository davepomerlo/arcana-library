::: note About this text
Freemasonry as an organised society of gentlemen dates from the founding of the Grand Lodge in London in 1717. But it grew out of the customs of working stonemasons, and the oldest evidence for those customs is a small parchment book in the old Royal Library, now in the British Library. It was catalogued for centuries as "a poem of moral duties", until James Orchard Halliwell, who was not a Freemason, published it in 1840 and pointed out what it was.

The poem is one of the "Old Charges": legendary histories of the craft followed by rules for masters and apprentices, which were read aloud when a new mason was admitted. It traces masonry to Euclid in Egypt and says the craft came to England under King Athelstan (924–939). The poet was probably a priest: near the end he says "when the gospel me rede schal". A close relative, the Cooke manuscript (British Library Additional MS 23198), dates from the 15th century.

Andrew Prescott, the first director of the University of Sheffield's Centre for Research into Freemasonry (2000–2007), begins his history of British Freemasonry in 1425 and has argued that it must be read in the light of wider history: "Freemasonry cannot be explained by Freemasonry." On that view the Old Charges are documents of a medieval craft organising itself, not evidence of an ancient secret tradition.
:::

## The founding of the craft

::: parallel 1–12
Whose wol bothe wel rede and loke,
He may fynde wryte yn olde boke
Of grete lordys and eke ladyysse,
That hade mony chyldryn y-fere, y-wisse;
And hade no rentys to fynde hem wyth,
Nowther yn towne, ny felde, ny fryth:
A cownsel togeder they cowthe hem take,
To ordeyne for these chyldryn sake,
How they my[g]th best lede here lyfe
Withoute gret desese, care and stryfe;
And most for the multytude that was comynge
Of here chyldryn after here [g]yndynge.
---
Whoever will both read well and look
may find written in an old book
of great lords and also ladies
who had many children together, indeed,
and had no income to provide for them,
neither in town, nor field, nor wood.
They took counsel together
to make provision for these children's sake,
how they might best lead their lives
without great hardship, care and strife,
and most of all for the multitude that would come
of their children after their own end.
:::

::: parallel 13–25
(They) sende thenne after grete clerkys,
To techyn hem thenne gode werkys;
And pray we hem, for our Lordys sake,
To oure chyldryn sum werke to make,
That they my[g]th gete here lyvynge therby,
Bothe wel and onestlyche, ful sycurly.
Yn that tyme, thro[g]gh good gemetry,
Thys onest craft of good masonry
Wes ordeynt and made yn thys manere,
Y-cownterfetyd of thys clerkys y-fere;
At these lordys prayers they cownterfetyd gemetry,
And [g]af hyt the name of masonry,
For the moste oneste craft of alle.
---
They sent then for great clerks
to teach them good works then:
"And we pray them, for our Lord's sake,
to devise some work for our children,
so that they might earn their living by it,
both well and honestly, in full security."
In that time, through good geometry,
this honest craft of good masonry
was ordained and made in this way,
devised by these clerks together:
at these lords' prayers they devised geometry
and gave it the name of masonry,
as the most honest craft of all.
:::

## The craft comes to England

::: parallel 61–66
Thys craft com ynto Englond, as y yow say,
Yn tyme of good kynge Adelstonus day;
He made tho bothe halle and eke bowre,
And hye templus of gret honowre,
To sportyn hym yn bothe day and ny[g]th,
An to worschepe hys God with alle hys my[g]th.
---
This craft came into England, as I tell you,
in the days of good King Athelstan.
He built then both hall and chamber,
and high temples of great honour,
to take his pleasure in both day and night,
and to worship his God with all his might.
:::

::: note Words worth knowing
- **cownterfetyd** (line 22): not "forged" but "devised, made in imitation of": the clerks shaped the craft on the model of geometry.
- **[g]yndynge** (line 12): *ȝendyng*, "ending", that is, death.
- **fryth** (line 6): woodland.
- **y-fere** (lines 4, 22): together.
:::
