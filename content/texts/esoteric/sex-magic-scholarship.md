::: note Scope of this guide
This page covers the history of an idea and the people who developed it, as studied by academic historians of religion. It does not describe practices. The subject has attracted more sensational writing than almost any other in esotericism, so this guide relies only on peer-reviewed or university-press scholarship.
:::

## An old accusation, a modern practice

For most of European history, "sexual magic" was something people were *accused* of. Heresy trials, witch trials and polemics against groups such as the Templars routinely charged them with secret sexual rites. Hugh Urban, in the first scholarly history of the subject (2006), argues that these charges tell us about the accusers' fears, not about real practice. A literature written by people who actually advocated sex as a source of spiritual or magical power appears only in the 19th century.

## Paschal Beverly Randolph (1825–1875)

The founder of modern Western sex magic, in the judgment of both Urban and John Patrick Deveney, was **Paschal Beverly Randolph**, an African American born in New York's Five Points slum. Self-educated, he became a trance medium, one of the first Black American novelists, a recruiter of Black soldiers for the Union army and a teacher of freed people in Louisiana during the Civil War. He founded a Rosicrucian order and taught that sexual union, approached with the right intention, could be a means of prayer and of spiritual power.

Deveney's biography (SUNY Press, 1997) was the first scholarly study of Randolph. It shows how he helped turn Spiritualism, the passive receiving of messages from the dead, into occultism, the active pursuit of inner vision. His teachings left traces on later occultists, including Madame Blavatsky and her Theosophical Society, and are still practised by small orders that claim descent from him. Deveney's book prints the full text of Randolph's two main manuscripts on the subject.

## Crowley, Tantra and the 20th century

Urban follows the story through a series of figures, chapter by chapter:

- **Aleister Crowley** (1875–1947), who made "sex magick" a central secret of the Ordo Templi Orientis and his religion of Thelema;
- European writers who borrowed, and often misread, **Indian Tantra**, which they knew mainly through Orientalist books and the *Kāma Sūtra*;
- **Julius Evola** (1898–1974), whose "yoga of power" tied sexual metaphysics to fascism;
- **Gerald Gardner** (1884–1964) and the Wiccan "Great Rite", and the feminist neo-pagan revival;
- **Anton LaVey** (1930–1997) and the Church of Satan, and finally chaos magic and the internet.

His argument is that modern sex magic is less a survival of ancient secrets than a product of modernity itself. It grew alongside 19th- and 20th-century debates over birth control, "sexual deviance", homosexuality and feminism, and became a language for personal liberation.

## Tantra: a necessary caution

Popular books often equate Western sex magic with Tantra. Scholars of South Asian religion stress that historical Tantra is a vast body of ritual and meditation traditions, Hindu, Buddhist and Jain, in which sexual rites play a limited and contested role, often in symbolic or internalised form. Western "Tantra", as Urban showed in earlier work, is largely a modern reinterpretation. The archive's Library lists critical editions of Tantric texts for readers who want the primary sources.
