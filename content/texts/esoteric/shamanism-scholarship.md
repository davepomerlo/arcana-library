::: note About this guide
"Shaman" is one of the most used and least agreed-upon words in the study of religion. This guide traces how the idea was built, from 16th-century travellers' reports to modern workshops, using the work of historians and anthropologists.
:::

## The word

*Shaman* comes from the Evenki (Tungus) language of eastern Siberia, where it named a ritual specialist. It reached Western Europe through Russian. The Lübeck merchant **Adam Brand**, in his 1698 account of a Russian embassy to China, introduced it to German and, in translation the same year, to English readers. Some scholars have linked the word to a Tungus root meaning "to know"; another theory, now less favoured, derives it from the Sanskrit *śramaṇa*, a wandering ascetic, via Buddhism.

## The first descriptions

- **Richard Johnson (1557)**, an English traveller with the Muscovy Company, left the earliest European account of a Siberian ritual specialist, among the Samoyeds.
- **Archpriest Avvakum**, the Old Believer exiled to Siberia in the 1650s, described in his autobiography (written 1669–75) an Evenki shaman performing a divination before a Russian military expedition. He saw it as devil worship. One historian calls his account the foundation of what anthropologists would later name the study of shamanism.
- **Nicolaes Witsen**, a Dutch statesman, published in 1692 the first European picture of a Siberian shaman, with drum and antlers, labelled a "priest of the devil".

Most 18th-century observers, scientists sent by the Russian state, dismissed shamans as tricksters. The Romantic era reversed the judgment.

## Eliade's universal shaman

In 1951 the Romanian historian of religion **Mircea Eliade** published *Shamanism: Archaic Techniques of Ecstasy*. He defined the shaman as a master of ecstasy who controls spirits and journeys to other worlds, and found this figure across Siberia, the Americas, Australia and ancient Eurasia. His book made shamanism a universal and very ancient religion, and inspired the "core shamanism" taught by the anthropologist Michael Harner from the 1980s, and much of modern neoshamanism.

## The critics

**Ronald Hutton**, a historian at the University of Bristol, went back to the Siberian evidence in *Shamans: Siberian Spirituality and the Western Imagination* (2001). He shows that:

- Siberian "shamans" served different peoples in very different ways: healers, diviners, funeral guides, sometimes possessed by spirits rather than controlling them, contrary to Eliade's model;
- what Europeans knew about them was shaped by Russian colonial rule, by Soviet persecution and by the observers' own assumptions;
- the idea of a single, worldwide shamanism was built by Western scholars from very mixed evidence.

The anthropologist **Alice Kehoe** goes further in *Shamans and Religion* (2000), arguing that the word should be kept for Siberia and that applying it to Native American and other Indigenous traditions erases their differences and invites cultural appropriation.

## Where this leaves the word

Most scholars now use "shamanism" carefully: as a convenient label for practices of trance, healing and spirit contact, not as one religion. Many Indigenous Siberians, after decades of Soviet suppression, have revived shamanic practice in their own terms since the 1990s, alongside a global neoshamanic movement.
