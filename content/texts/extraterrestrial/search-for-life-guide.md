## From philosophy to astronomy

The idea that there are other worlds is ancient. The Greek atomist **Epicurus** argued around 300 BCE that infinite atoms must make [infinitely many worlds](epicurus-infinite-worlds.html); the Roman poet **Lucretius** set the argument in [verse](lucretius-other-worlds.html); and in 1584 **Giordano Bruno** made the stars into suns with [inhabited worlds of their own](bruno-infinite-worlds-1584.html). In 1758 the Swedish visionary Emanuel Swedenborg described the inhabitants of the planets as he said spirits had told him (see [contact claims](contact-claims-guide.html)).

By the late nineteenth century, telescopes had turned the question to Mars, and the supposed "canals" made Martians a popular expectation. The first claims of signals came with radio itself: Nikola Tesla reported in 1899 a repeating signal he later attributed to Mars, and Guglielmo Marconi believed in 1921 that he had picked up a Martian reply. Neither claim was ever confirmed.

## 1959: a proposal to listen

In September 1959 two Cornell physicists, **Giuseppe Cocconi** and **Philip Morrison**, published a two-page paper in *Nature*, "Searching for Interstellar Communications". They argued that a civilisation trying to make contact would use radio, and that both sides would choose the same obvious frequency: **1420 MHz**, the 21-centimetre line at which the universe's commonest element, neutral hydrogen, naturally radiates. They named nearby Sun-like stars, including Tau Ceti and Epsilon Eridani, as first targets. Their closing line became the motto of the field: "if we never search, the chance of success is zero."

## 1960–61: Project Ozma and the Drake equation

Working independently, the radio astronomer **Frank Drake** pointed the 26-metre (85-foot) dish at Green Bank, West Virginia, at Tau Ceti and Epsilon Eridani in 1960, listening near 1420 MHz for about four months. This was **Project Ozma**, the first modern search. A signal on 8 April 1960 caused brief excitement; it turned out to come from an aircraft.

In 1961 Drake hosted the first scientific meeting on the subject at Green Bank, with ten participants including Morrison, the chemist Melvin Calvin, the young Carl Sagan and the dolphin researcher John Lilly (after whose work the group jokingly called itself "the Order of the Dolphin"). To give the meeting an agenda, Drake wrote down the factors that would decide how many communicating civilisations exist in our galaxy:

**N = R\* · fₚ · nₑ · fₗ · fᵢ · f꜀ · L**

— the rate of star formation, the fraction of stars with planets, the number of habitable planets per system, and the fractions on which life, intelligence and detectable technology arise, multiplied by how long such civilisations last. The **Drake equation** is not a measurement: the last four terms are unknown, and estimates of *N* range from one (ourselves) to millions. Its value lies in showing what we would need to learn.

## Signals and searches

- **Other strategies.** In 1960 Ronald Bracewell suggested looking for robotic probes in our own solar system; in 1961 a *Nature* paper proposed searching for laser pulses. In 1971 NASA's Project Cyclops study proposed searching the quiet band between the hydrogen line (1420 MHz) and the hydroxyl lines (around 1660 MHz), the "water hole".
- **The Wow! signal (1977).** On 15 August 1977 Ohio State University's Big Ear radio telescope recorded a strong, narrow-band signal close to 1420 MHz. The volunteer astronomer Jerry Ehman circled it on the printout and wrote "Wow!". It lasted about as long as a source would take to drift through the telescope's beam, and has never been seen again despite many attempts. It remains unexplained but unconfirmed, which in science means it cannot count as a detection.
- **Breakthrough Listen (2015).** On 20 July 2015, at the Royal Society in London, the investor Yuri Milner, with Stephen Hawking, announced a $100 million, ten-year programme based at the University of California, Berkeley. It bought time on the Green Bank Telescope and the Parkes radio telescope in Australia, and on an optical telescope searching for lasers, to survey the million nearest stars, the plane and centre of the Milky Way and a hundred nearby galaxies. Its results go through ordinary peer review. It has reported no confirmed signal; its organisers' stated aim, even without a detection, was to set the most rigorous limits yet on how common powerful transmitters can be.

## Visitors from other stars

- **1I/ʻOumuamua (2017).** On 19 October 2017 Robert Weryk, working with the Pan-STARRS1 survey telescope on Haleakalā, Hawaii, found a small, fast object on an orbit that could not be bound to the Sun. It was the first object known to have come from another star system. Its Hawaiian name, chosen with local language experts, means roughly "a messenger from afar arriving first". Its elongated shape and a slight acceleration not explained by gravity alone made it a puzzle, and prompted the suggestion that it might be artificial. Most researchers favour natural explanations.
- **2I/Borisov (2019).** The second interstellar object, an ordinary-looking comet.
- **3I/ATLAS (2025).** Discovered on 1 July 2025 by the NASA-funded ATLAS survey telescope at Río Hurtado, Chile, the third interstellar object is a comet on a strongly hyperbolic path (eccentricity about 6). It is active like other comets, with a dusty coma, and some estimates suggest it may be older than the Solar System.

## Biosignatures: life without technology

Most astronomers now think the first evidence of life elsewhere, if it comes, will be chemical — a gas in a planet's atmosphere that is hard to explain without living things.

**K2-18 b**, a planet larger than Earth about 124 light-years away, became the test case. In 2023 and again in April 2025, a team led by **Nikku Madhusudhan** of the University of Cambridge reported hints in James Webb Space Telescope data of **dimethyl sulfide (DMS)**, a gas that on Earth is produced by marine life. The April 2025 result was stated at about three standard deviations ("3-sigma"), well short of the five-sigma threshold usually required for a discovery.

Several independent reanalyses followed in 2025, including one by Rafael Luque and colleagues at the University of Chicago, one by Luis Welbanks and colleagues, and a NASA-led study. They found no statistically significant evidence for DMS: with more complete models, other molecules fit the data as well. Madhusudhan's team agrees the evidence is moderate, not a detection, and continues to regard DMS as a promising candidate. The status as of late 2026 is **disputed, not confirmed**. The episode recalls the 1996 claim of fossil microbes in the Martian meteorite ALH 84001, which most scientists now think can be explained without life.

## What the evidence shows

- **No confirmed detection.** Six decades of radio and optical searches, and the first atmospheric studies of small exoplanets, have found no confirmed sign of life or technology elsewhere.
- **The silence is informative but not decisive.** The searches have covered only a tiny fraction of the stars, frequencies and kinds of signal possible. The limits they set are real; the question is open.
- **The ancient premise has been vindicated in one respect.** Planets are common: thousands are now known around other stars. Whether any of them is inhabited is exactly the question Epicurus raised, now posed in a form that can be answered by measurement.
