::: note About this text
Epicurus (341–270 BCE) taught in his Garden at Athens that everything is made of atoms moving in empty space. Almost all of his writing is lost. Three letters survive because the biographer Diogenes Laertius copied them into the tenth book of his *Lives of Eminent Philosophers*. The *Letter to Herodotus* (not the historian) is a summary of Epicurus' physics for students.

In section 45, Epicurus draws a conclusion from two earlier claims: that the universe is unlimited, and that the atoms in it are unlimited in number. If there are endless atoms, they cannot all have been used up in making one world. So there must be endless worlds.

A *kosmos* here does not mean a planet. It means an ordered system — an earth with its sky, sun, moon and stars — set in the larger, boundless "All". Later Epicureans called the spaces between such systems *metakosmia*, in Latin *intermundia*. Whether other worlds are *inhabited* is a further step. This passage does not take it; Lucretius, writing two centuries later, does, a few lines after the [passage given in this archive](lucretius-other-worlds.html).
:::

::: parallel 45 (1–2)
Ἀλλὰ μὴν καὶ κόσμοι ἄπειροί εἰσιν, οἵ θ᾽ ὅμοιοι τούτῳ καὶ ἀνόμοιοι.
αἵ τε γὰρ ἄτομοι ἄπειροι οὖσαι, ὡς ἄρτι ἀπεδείχθη, φέρονται καὶ πορρωτάτω.
---
Moreover, there are infinitely many worlds, both like this one and unlike it.
For the atoms, being infinite in number, as was shown just now, are carried even to the farthest distances.
:::

::: parallel 45 (3–5)
οὐ γὰρ κατανήλωνται αἱ τοιαῦται ἄτομοι, ἐξ ὧν ἂν γένοιτο κόσμος ἢ ὑφ᾽ ὧν ἂν ποιηθείη,
οὔτ᾽ εἰς ἕνα οὔτ᾽ εἰς πεπερασμένους, οὔθ᾽ ὅσοι τοιοῦτοι οὔθ᾽ ὅσοι διάφοροι τούτοις.
ὥστε οὐδὲν τὸ ἐμποδοστατῆσόν ἐστι πρὸς τὴν ἀπειρίαν τῶν κόσμων.
---
For such atoms — those from which a world could come to be, or by which one could be made — have not been used up,
either on one world or on a limited number of worlds, neither on all the worlds that are like this one nor on those that differ from them.
So there is nothing that stands in the way of an infinity of worlds.
:::

::: note Words worth knowing
**ἄπειρος** (*apeiros*), "infinite": literally "without a limit" (*a-* "not" + *peras* "end, boundary").

**κόσμος** (*kosmos*): "order, arrangement", then "the ordered world". Epicurus' plural *kosmoi* means world-systems, not merely other planets.

**ἐμποδοστατῆσον** (*empodostatēson*): "that which will stand in the way", a rare, vivid word for an obstacle.
:::

## Why it matters

Plato and Aristotle had argued that there is only one world. Epicurus' reply became the root of the "plurality of worlds" tradition. Lucretius set it in Latin verse ([*De rerum natura* 2.1048–1066](lucretius-other-worlds.html)); Giordano Bruno revived it in 1584, adding that the stars are suns ([*La cena de le ceneri*](bruno-infinite-worlds-1584.html)); and modern astronomy, with thousands of known exoplanets, has turned it from speculation into a question of measurement.
