::: note About this text
Giordano Bruno (1548–1600) was a Dominican friar from Nola, near Naples, who left his order and spent years wandering through France, England and Germany. In London in 1584, under the protection of the French ambassador, he published his great Italian dialogues. Two of them concern the cosmos.

***La cena de le ceneri*** ("The Ash Wednesday Supper") defends Copernicus, who had put the Sun rather than the Earth at the centre. Bruno goes much further. In the letter that opens the book, where he sets out the arguments of the dialogues point by point, he says that the universe is infinite and has no centre at all, and that the stars are worlds made of the same matter as ours.

***De l'infinito, universo e mondi*** ("On the Infinite, the Universe and Worlds"), printed the same year, develops the argument in full.

Bruno was arrested by the Venetian Inquisition in 1592, handed over to Rome, and after a trial lasting years was burned alive in the Campo de' Fiori on 17 February 1600. His cosmology was one of many charges; the main ones concerned theology. He is not a martyr of science in any simple sense — his reasons were as much Hermetic and theological as astronomical — but his picture of an endless universe full of suns and inhabited worlds was strikingly modern.
:::

::: parallel La cena de le ceneri, prefatory letter (on the universe)
[…] dechiara essere infinita la mole de l'universo, e che invano si cerca il centro o la circonferenza del mondo universale […]
---
[…] he declares that the bulk of the universe is infinite, and that it is useless to look for the centre or the circumference of the universal world […]
:::

::: parallel La cena de le ceneri, prefatory letter (on other worlds)
La quarta afferma esser conformi in materia questo mondo nostro, ch'è detto globo della terra, con gli mondi, che son gli corpi degli altri astri; e che è cosa da fanciulli aver creduto, e credere, altrimente; e che quei son tanti animali intellettuali; e che non meno in quelli vegetano ed intendono molti ed innumerabili individui semplici e composti, che veggiamo vivere e vegetar nel dorso di questo.
---
The fourth affirms that this world of ours, which is called the globe of the earth, is made of the same matter as the worlds that are the bodies of the other stars; that it is childish to have believed, or to believe, otherwise; that those worlds are so many intelligent living beings; and that on them, no less than on this one, many and innumerable individuals, simple and compound, grow and understand, just as we see them live and grow on the back of this one.
:::

::: parallel De l'infinito, universo e mondi, dedicatory letter
Or ecco, vi porgo la mia contemplazione circa l'infinito, universo e mondi innumerabili.
---
Here, then, I offer you my contemplation of the infinite, the universe and innumerable worlds.
:::

::: note Words worth knowing
**animali intellettuali**: "intelligent animals", that is, living, thinking beings. For Bruno the stars and planets are themselves alive, each with a soul, as in the Platonic and Hermetic tradition he admired.

**vegetano ed intendono**: "grow and understand", from the old division of the soul into vegetative, sensitive and intellectual powers. Bruno means that other worlds have both living things and minds.
:::
