::: note About this text
Lucretius wrote *De rerum natura*, "On the Nature of Things", in the mid first century BCE. It is a poem in six books that sets out the physics of Epicurus, whom Lucretius reveres, so that its reader — a Roman aristocrat named Memmius — might be freed from the fear of the gods and of death. Almost nothing is known of the poet's life.

Near the end of Book 2, Lucretius has shown that space is infinite and atoms innumerable. He now draws the conclusion. Our world was not designed; it formed by chance, when atoms collided and happened to stick together. Since the same causes are at work everywhere in an endless universe, the same result must happen elsewhere. A few lines after this passage he goes further, saying other earths must have their own races of people and kinds of animals.
:::

::: parallel 1048–1051
Principio nobis in cunctas undique partis
et latere ex utroque supterque per omne
nulla est finis; uti docui, res ipsaque per se
vociferatur, et elucet natura profundi.
---
First of all: for us, in every direction all around,
on either side, above and below, through the whole,
there is no limit — as I have taught, and as the thing itself
cries aloud, and the nature of the deep shines clear.
:::

::: parallel 1052–1057
nullo iam pacto veri simile esse putandumst,
undique cum vorsum spatium vacet infinitum
seminaque innumero numero summaque profunda
multimodis volitent aeterno percita motu,
hunc unum terrarum orbem caelumque creatum,
nil agere illa foris tot corpora materiai;
---
So in no way can it be thought likely —
when space lies open and unbounded on every side,
and seeds beyond number, in a sum without bottom,
fly about in many ways, driven by everlasting motion —
that this one round of earth and this one sky were made,
and that all those bodies of matter outside do nothing;
:::

::: parallel 1058–1063
cum praesertim hic sit natura factus et ipsa
sponte sua forte offensando semina rerum
multimodis temere in cassum frustraque coacta
tandem coluerunt ea quae coniecta repente
magnarum rerum fierent exordia semper,
terrai maris et caeli generisque animantum.
---
especially since this world was made by nature: the seeds of things
themselves, colliding by chance of their own accord,
driven together in many ways, at random, uselessly and in vain,
at last combined — those which, suddenly thrown together,
would become each time the beginnings of great things:
of earth, of sea and sky, and of the race of living creatures.
:::

::: parallel 1064–1066
quare etiam atque etiam talis fateare necesse est
esse alios alibi congressus materiai,
qualis hic est, avido complexu quem tenet aether.
---
Therefore, again and again, you must admit
that there are elsewhere other gatherings of matter
like this one, which the aether holds in its greedy embrace.
:::

::: note Words worth knowing
**semina rerum**, "seeds of things": one of Lucretius' Latin names for atoms. He avoids the Greek word *atomos*.

**congressus materiai**, "a coming-together of matter": a world, seen as a temporary cluster of atoms. *Materiai* is the old genitive form of *materia*, kept for the metre.

**aether**: the bright upper air or sky that surrounds our world.
:::

## Afterlife of the passage

The poem was nearly lost. A manuscript was found in a German monastery in 1417 by the Italian humanist Poggio Bracciolini, and the work then circulated in print. Giordano Bruno knew it well; his dialogues of 1584 argue, as Lucretius did, that the universe is infinite and that there are innumerable worlds ([read Bruno](bruno-infinite-worlds-1584.html)).
