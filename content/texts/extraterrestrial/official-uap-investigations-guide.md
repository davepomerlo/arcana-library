::: note How to read this guide
"UFO" (unidentified flying object) and the newer official term "UAP" (first *unidentified aerial*, now *unidentified anomalous*, phenomena) mean only that the observer could not identify what was seen. Neither term implies anything extraterrestrial. Each investigation below had its own brief: some asked "is this a threat?", others "is there anything here for science?". Their conclusions answer those questions, not every question people have asked about UFOs. For the older history of lights in the sky, see the case files in the [Paranormal section](../sections/paranormal.html).
:::

## United States Air Force: Sign, Grudge and Blue Book (1947–1969)

The Air Force's investigation began in 1948 as **Project Sign**, became **Project Grudge**, and from 1952–53 was **Project Blue Book**, run from Wright-Patterson Air Force Base in Ohio.

- **What it found.** Of 12,618 sightings reported between 1947 and 1969, 701 remained "unidentified". The rest were put down to aircraft, balloons, satellites, stars and planets, weather, hoaxes and similar causes.
- **Conclusion.** No UFO had threatened national security, none showed technology beyond what was then known, and there was "no evidence indicating that sightings categorized as 'unidentified' are extraterrestrial vehicles" (Air Force fact sheet).
- **End.** The Secretary of the Air Force closed Blue Book on 17 December 1969, after the Condon Report (below).
- **Records.** The declassified files are in the U.S. National Archives, which also holds related radar, film and audiovisual records.

## The CIA's Robertson Panel (January 1953)

After a wave of sightings over Washington, D.C., in the summer of 1952, the CIA's Office of Scientific Intelligence convened a panel of scientists. It was chaired by the physicist **H. P. Robertson**, with Samuel Goudsmit, Luis Alvarez, Thornton Page and Lloyd Berkner. It met on 14–17 January 1953 and reviewed seventy-five of the best-documented cases from 1951–52.

- **Conclusion.** The evidence showed "no indication that these phenomena constitute a direct physical threat to national security."
- **The controversial part.** The panel worried that a flood of reports could clog military communications, and recommended public education to reduce interest in the subject. When this became known, critics read it as a call to debunk, and the panel's secrecy fuelled suspicion of a cover-up for decades. Both the report and the fuller minutes by its secretary, F. C. Durant, are now public.

## The Condon Report and the National Academy of Sciences (1966–1969)

The Air Force paid the University of Colorado to run an independent study, under contract F44620-67-C-0035. The physicist **Edward U. Condon** was scientific director and Daniel S. Gillmor editor. The final report, *Scientific Study of Unidentified Flying Objects*, was sent to the Air Force on 31 October 1968 and is nearly 1,500 pages long.

- **Conclusion.** Condon's summary held that two decades of UFO study had added nothing to scientific knowledge and that further extensive study was unlikely to do so, although some individual cases in the report remained unexplained.
- **Review.** A panel of the National Academy of Sciences, set up in late 1968, concurred: it judged that a study of UFOs in general was not a promising way to expand scientific understanding.
- **Criticism.** The astronomer J. Allen Hynek, a long-time Blue Book consultant, and others argued that the summary did not reflect the unexplained cases inside the report. The physicist Peter Sturrock later published a detailed analysis making the same point. The report remains the reference point for every later debate.

## United Kingdom: the Ministry of Defence UFO files (1950s–2009)

The Ministry of Defence (MoD) collected UFO reports for over fifty years. In 2008 it began releasing its files through The National Archives; the tenth and final tranche came out on 21 June 2013. That last release alone contained 25 files and about 4,400 pages, covering the final two years of the MoD's UFO desk, from late 2007 until it closed in November 2009.

- **Conclusion.** A 2009 briefing to ministers said that in more than fifty years, no UFO sighting reported to the department had revealed anything to suggest an extraterrestrial presence or a military threat to the UK, and that there was no defence benefit in investigating them.
- **The rise in reports.** Reports ran at about 150 a year until 2007, roughly doubled in 2008 and passed 600 in 2009, as the files were being published. The historian David Clarke, who advised The National Archives, took this as evidence that UFOs are very much a social phenomenon.

## France: GEIPAN at the French space agency (1977–today)

France is the only country with a permanent civilian UAP office inside its space agency. Founded by CNES in 1977 as GEPAN, it became SEPRA in 1988 and has been GEIPAN since 2005. In 2007 it became the first such office to put its case archive online, with witnesses' names removed.

- **Method.** Reports come from the public, the gendarmerie, the police, civil aviation and the military. GEIPAN investigates, consults a panel of outside experts on difficult cases, and publishes each case with a classification.
- **Results.** By CNES's figures, 24.6% of phenomena are clearly identified, 39.7% probably identified, 32.4% unidentified for lack of data, and 3.3% unidentified after investigation.
- **What it is not.** GEIPAN states plainly that it is not a research group on UFOs and extraterrestrial life. Its job is to explain what people saw, and to archive what cannot yet be explained.

## ODNI's Preliminary Assessment (2021)

Congress asked the Director of National Intelligence for a report on UAP reported by the U.S. military. The nine-page unclassified *Preliminary Assessment: Unidentified Aerial Phenomena* was released on 25 June 2021.

- **Data.** 144 reports from U.S. government sources, mostly Navy aviators, dated November 2004 to March 2021; 80 of them involved more than one sensor.
- **Findings.** Only one was explained with high confidence (a large, deflating balloon). The report proposed five likely categories for the rest: airborne clutter such as birds and balloons, natural atmospheric phenomena, U.S. government or industry development programmes, foreign adversary systems, and an "other" bin. It said that the limited amount of high-quality reporting hampered firm conclusions, and noted that a small number of reports seemed to show unusual movement that needed more analysis.
- **Effect.** It treated UAP as a flight-safety and possibly national-security issue, and led to a permanent office: AARO.

## AARO's Historical Record Report, Volume I (2024)

The Pentagon's All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) was directed by Congress to review the whole government record since 1945. Volume I, 63 pages, was released on 8 March 2024 and covers the period to 31 October 2023. AARO reviewed earlier programmes, searched classified and unclassified archives, and interviewed some thirty people, including those who said they had knowledge of secret programmes.

- **Conclusion.** AARO found no verifiable evidence that any UAP sighting represented extraterrestrial activity, and no verifiable evidence that the U.S. government or private industry has ever had access to extraterrestrial technology. Most sightings it reviewed were misidentified ordinary objects and phenomena.
- **New detail.** The report described a proposed programme called KONA BLUE, intended to study recovered off-world technology, which the Department of Homeland Security considered and rejected; it never went beyond a proposal.
- **Dispute.** The report rejects the claims made to Congress in July 2023 by David Grusch, a former intelligence officer, that the government holds recovered non-human craft. Grusch and others say AARO did not have access to the programmes concerned. A second volume, covering material received after October 2023, was announced; check AARO's site for its status.

## NASA's UAP Independent Study Team (2022–2023)

NASA commissioned sixteen outside experts, chaired by the astrophysicist **David Spergel**, to advise how NASA could contribute to the study of UAP using unclassified and civilian data. The team began work in October 2022, held a public meeting on 31 May 2023, and published its report on 14 September 2023. Its members reported harassment and threats during the study.

- **Conclusion.** The team found no evidence that UAP have an extraterrestrial origin. Its main finding was that the data are poor: few reports have calibrated measurements from more than one sensor.
- **Recommendations.** NASA should take a prominent part in a whole-of-government effort built on systematic, open data collection, including gathering reports from commercial pilots through the Federal Aviation Administration. NASA appointed a Director of UAP Research the same day.

## What the investigations agree on

- **Most reports are explained.** In every large data set — Blue Book, the MoD files, GEIPAN — the great majority of sightings turn out to be aircraft, balloons, satellites, astronomical objects, weather or hoaxes.
- **A remainder stays unexplained.** It is usually small (about 3% in GEIPAN's current statistics) and is almost always unexplained *for lack of data* rather than because the data show something impossible.
- **No investigation has found evidence of extraterrestrial visitors.** That is a finding about the evidence presented to them, not a proof that nothing remains to be found.
- **Secrecy did harm.** The Robertson Panel's classified recommendations and decades of withheld files did more than any sighting to create the belief in a cover-up. Every recent inquiry, from the UK release to NASA's open meeting, has tried to answer that with publication.
