::: note Why this matters
Most of what we can read from antiquity survived because it was copied again and again. The finds below are different: texts that dropped out of the copying tradition and came back from the ground, from a tomb, or from inside a carbonised scroll. Several have changed what scholars think about the origins of the traditions in this archive. Some were only translated in full into English in the last twenty years.
:::

## Gnostic and early Christian

**Nag Hammadi codices (found 1945, Egypt).** Thirteen leather-bound papyrus books, buried in the 4th century, containing 52 mostly Gnostic tractates in Coptic, among them the complete *Gospel of Thomas*. A full English translation appeared in 1977. See [the Gospel of Thomas](gospel-of-thomas-selections.html).

**Codex Tchacos and the Gospel of Judas (published 2006).** A 4th-century Coptic codex that surfaced on the antiquities market in the 1970s and spent years deteriorating in a bank safe in New York before it was restored. Its *Gospel of Judas*, known until then only because the bishop Irenaeus attacked it around 180 CE, was published in English by the National Geographic Society in 2006, edited by Rodolphe Kasser, Marvin Meyer and Gregor Wurst. Its interpretation (is Judas a hero or a demon?) is still disputed.

## Chinese bamboo and silk

**Mawangdui (excavated 1973).** A Han tomb sealed in 168 BCE held silk manuscripts including two copies of the *Laozi* older than any previously known, with the two halves in reverse order, as well as texts on medicine, breathing exercises and astronomy.

**Guodian (excavated 1993).** A Chu tomb of about 300 BCE held bamboo slips with the oldest known *Laozi* material and the previously unknown cosmogony *Taiyi sheng shui*, "The Great One Gave Birth to Water". Scott Cook's complete annotated translation of the Guodian texts appeared in 2012. See [Taiyi sheng shui](taiyi-sheng-shui.html).

**Shanghai Museum slips (purchased 1994)** and **Tsinghua slips (donated 2008).** Two large collections of looted Warring States bamboo slips, bought on the Hong Kong market and given to Chinese institutions. Carbon dating of the Tsinghua slips gave about 305 BCE. Their first volume was published in 2010, and they include lost chapters of the *Book of Documents*.

**The Huainanzi (complete English translation 2010).** Not a find but a rediscovery: this Han compendium of 139 BCE, a key source for Chinese cosmology, had never been fully translated into English until John Major and colleagues did so in 2010. See [Heavenly Patterns](huainanzi-heavenly-patterns.html).

## Greek

**The Derveni papyrus (found 1962, published 2006).** The charred remains of a scroll found on a funeral pyre near Thessaloniki, from the 4th century BCE: a commentary on an Orphic poem about the origin of the gods, read in the light of Presocratic physics. Its official first edition (by Kouremenos, Parássoglou and Tsantsanoglou) appeared only in 2006, more than forty years after the find. UNESCO added it to its Memory of the World register in 2015 as the oldest surviving European book. See [the Orphic gold tablet](orphic-gold-tablet-petelia.html).

**Herculaneum (read 2023–2024).** The eruption of Vesuvius in 79 CE carbonised a library of some 800 papyrus rolls in a villa at Herculaneum. Unrolling them destroys them. In the Vesuvius Challenge, launched in 2023 by Nat Friedman, Daniel Gross and Brent Seales (University of Kentucky), contestants used X-ray scans and machine learning to read inside a still-rolled scroll. The first word found, in October 2023, was *πορφύρας*, "purple". In February 2024 Youssef Nader, Luke Farritor and Julian Schilliger won the $700,000 grand prize for revealing more than 2,000 letters in 15 partial columns of the scroll PHerc. Paris 4, apparently a work on pleasure by the Epicurean philosopher Philodemus.

**Plotinus, Enneads (complete new English translation 2018).** Cambridge University Press published a new complete translation edited by Lloyd Gerson, the first full English version since A. H. Armstrong's Loeb volumes (1966–1988). See [Plotinus on Beauty](plotinus-on-beauty.html).

## Mesopotamian

**A new piece of Gilgamesh (acquired 2011, published 2014).** The Sulaymaniyah Museum in Iraqi Kurdistan bought a clay tablet (T.1447) that turned out to be part of Tablet V of the Standard Babylonian *Epic of Gilgamesh*. Published by Farouk Al-Rawi and Andrew George in 2014, it adds about twenty lines to the episode of the Cedar Forest and shows it not as silent wilderness but as a noisy jungle, full of birdsong, cicadas and chattering monkeys.

::: note How to read new finds critically
- **Provenance.** The Shanghai and Tsinghua slips, Codex Tchacos and the Gilgamesh tablet all came through the antiquities market rather than a controlled excavation. That raises ethical questions about looting and practical ones about forgery; each has been accepted by specialists, but the debate is part of their story.
- **First editions take time.** The gap between a find and a reliable edition can be decades (Derveni: 44 years). Early popular translations are often revised.
- **"Lost gospel" headlines.** A new text tells us what some group believed at some date. It does not by itself show that its claims are older or truer than the canonical ones.
:::
