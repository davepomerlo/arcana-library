::: note About the text
*Enūma eliš* is named, like most Mesopotamian works, after its first words, “When on high”. It was recited in Babylon on the fourth day of the New Year festival (*akītu*). The story moves from these first births to the war between the young gods and Tiāmat, and ends with Marduk's kingship. The first tablets to be found were excavated at Nineveh in the 1840s–50s and published by George Smith in 1876.
:::

::: parallel 1–2
enūma eliš lā nabû šamāmū
šapliš ammatum šuma lā zakrat
---
When on high the heavens had not been named,
and below, the firm ground had not been called by name,
:::

::: parallel 3–5
Apsûma rēštû zārûšun
mummu Tiāmat muallidat gimrīšun
mêšunu ištēniš iḫīqūma
---
there was Apsû, the first, their begetter,
and maker Tiāmat, who bore them all;
they were mingling their waters together.
:::

::: parallel 6
gipāra lā kiṣṣurū ṣuṣâ lā šeʾû
---
No reed-bed had been knotted together, no marsh had been found.
:::

::: parallel 7–9
enūma ilū lā šūpû manāma
šuma lā zukkurū šīmātu lā šīmū
ibbanûma ilū qerebšun
---
When none of the gods had yet appeared,
none named with a name, no destinies decreed,
then the gods were formed within them.
:::

::: parallel 10
Laḫmu u Laḫāmu uštāpû šuma izzakrū
---
Laḫmu and Laḫāmu were brought forth; they were called by name.
:::

::: parallel 11–12
adi irbû išiḫḫû
Anšar u Kišar ibbanû elīšunu atrū
---
While they grew and became tall,
Anšar and Kišar were formed, surpassing them.
:::

::: parallel 13–14
urrikū ūmī uṣṣipū šanāti
Anu aplašunu šānin abbīšu
---
They made the days long and added the years;
Anu was their heir, the rival of his fathers.
:::

::: parallel 15–16
Anšar Anu bukrašu umaššilma
u Anu tamšīlašu ūlid Nudimmud
---
Anšar made Anu, his firstborn, his equal,
and Anu begot in his likeness Nudimmud (Ea).
:::

::: note Translation notes
**nabû / zakāru** both mean “to name, to call”. In Mesopotamian thought a thing did not fully exist until it was named, so the poem begins, like Genesis, with a world not yet spoken.

**Apsû** is the sweet underground water and **Tiāmat** is the sea; her name is the ordinary word *tâmtu*, “sea”. **mummu** may be an epithet of Tiāmat (“creator, maker”) or the name of a separate being, Apsû's vizier, who appears later in the poem.

**Line 6** is hard. *gipāru* is a reed-bed or pasture and *ṣuṣû* a marsh or canebrake. The line says that not even the most primitive marsh-land had yet formed.
:::
