::: note Why a guide, and why caution
Africa has thousands of peoples and languages, and their creation traditions were mostly **oral**. Apart from Egypt and Ethiopia, almost everything we can read was written down in the last two centuries, very often by European missionaries, colonial officers or anthropologists, who chose what to ask and how to translate. Some recorders printed the speakers' own words beside the translation; many did not. Before trusting a famous "African creation myth" found online, it is worth asking: who told it, to whom, in which language, and has anyone checked it since? This guide points to the sources that let you answer those questions.
:::

## Written in Africa, in African languages, before 1500

**Egypt (from c. 2350 BCE).** The oldest written creation accounts in the world: Atum emerging alone from the dark waters at Heliopolis and producing Shu and Tefnut (Pyramid Texts); Ptah creating by heart and tongue (the Memphite Theology on the Shabaka Stone); and the eight primeval gods of Hermopolis. See [Atum at Heliopolis](atum-heliopolis-creation.html).

**Ethiopia (Ge'ez).** The Ethiopian Orthodox Church preserved the *Book of Enoch* (*Mäṣḥafä Henok*) complete only in Ge'ez, the classical language of Ethiopia, including the story of the Watchers, angels who came down and taught forbidden arts to humans. It is a Jewish work of the last centuries BCE, but it survives as a whole because African scribes kept copying it.

## Recorded in the 19th–20th centuries with the original language

**Zulu (Natal, 1860s).** Henry Callaway's *Religious System of the Amazulu* prints Zulu narrators' own words beside his English: Unkulunkulu, the first ancestor, "broke off" from a bed of reeds; the chameleon and the lizard bring the messages of life and death. See [Unkulunkulu](zulu-unkulunkulu.html).

**San (/Xam, Cape Colony, 1870–1884).** Wilhelm Bleek and Lucy Lloyd recorded more than 12,000 pages of narratives from /Xam speakers, several of them prisoners released into their custody, including ||Kabbo and Diä!kwain. The selection published as *Specimens of Bushman Folklore* (1911) prints /Xam and English on facing pages, among them many stories of the trickster and creator figure the Mantis (/Kaggen). The /Xam language now has no speakers, and the archive is on UNESCO's Memory of the World register; the University of Cape Town has digitised it.

**Yoruba (Nigeria and the diaspora).** The creation of the earth is told in the verses (*ẹsẹ*) of the Ifá divination corpus: the god Ọbàtálá (or, in other versions, Odùduwà) descends from the sky on a chain with a shell of earth and a hen, which scatters the earth over the primeval water at Ile-Ife. The corpus has 256 *odù*, each with many verses, and is still growing; UNESCO recognised the Ifá system in 2005. Wande Abimbola's Yoruba and bilingual editions of Ifá verses (from the late 1960s) are the standard scholarly source.

## Recorded mainly in translation

**Kuba / Bushongo (Congo, 1907–1909).** Emil Torday and T. A. Joyce recorded the story of **Bumba**, who was alone in darkness over the water and, in pain, vomited up the sun, moon, stars, animals and finally people. Published in French (1910) and in *Folk-Lore* (1911), without the Kuba-language text.

**Dogon (Mali, 1946).** Marcel Griaule's *Dieu d'eau* (1948) presents the teachings of the blind elder **Ogotemmêli** over 33 days: a cosmos built from the word, the twin spirits Nommo, the cosmic egg. It became one of the most famous books on African thought. In 1991 Walter van Beek published a restudy in *Current Anthropology* reporting that the Dogon he worked with did not recognise most of it; Griaule's daughter Geneviève Calame-Griaule replied that van Beek's own informants and methods were the problem. The debate is unresolved. The claims, popular since the 1970s, that the Dogon had secret knowledge of the star Sirius B rest entirely on Griaule's later work and are not supported by van Beek's fieldwork.

## How to read these sources

- **Look for the original language.** Callaway, Bleek and Lloyd, and Abimbola let you check the translation. A story found only in English, with no named teller, is weaker evidence.
- **Look for the teller.** Many collections name their informants: ||Kabbo, Diä!kwain, Ogotemmêli. Their individual voices matter as much as "the tradition".
- **Expect several versions.** African traditions vary by town, lineage and teller. A single "Yoruba creation myth" or "Zulu creation myth" is a simplification.
- **Remember the colonial setting.** Missionaries often looked for a "High God" like their own, and translated accordingly. Modern African scholars, such as Jacob Olupona on the Yoruba and Ife, have reframed many of these questions.
