## North America: the Haudenosaunee Sky Woman

The Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) creation story tells how a woman fell from the Sky World through a hole beside an uprooted tree, was caught on the wings of birds and set down on the back of a great turtle, where the animals had brought up mud from beneath the water to make the earth ("Turtle Island"). Her grandsons, twins often called the Good Mind and the Bad Mind, shaped the world.

The most important early record is **J. N. B. Hewitt's *Iroquoian Cosmology*** (1903, second part 1928). Hewitt, who was of Tuscarora descent, published versions told by the Onondaga chief John Buck (1889), the Seneca John Armstrong (1896) and the Mohawk Seth Newhouse (1896–97) with close, literal English translations. The Smithsonian's scan of the 1903 report is the version to cite; the sacred-texts edition reproduces the English. Haudenosaunee nations today tell the story in their own ways, and many prefer that it be heard from their own speakers.

## Mesoamerica: the Legend of the Suns

The ***Leyenda de los Soles*** ("Legend of the Suns"), a Nahuatl text dated 1558, calls itself a *tlamachiliztlatolzazanilli*, a "wisdom-discourse tale". It tells of four earlier worlds, each destroyed — the first, "4 Jaguar", lasted 676 years before its people were eaten by jaguars — and of the present fifth sun, created when the gods sacrificed themselves at Teotihuacan. The manuscript survived only in the Codex Chimalpopoca, which has been missing since 1949, so scholars rely on a photographic facsimile. The standard edition is **John Bierhorst's** (1992), with the Nahuatl text and English translation in companion volumes.

## Ainu Mosir: kamuy yukar

**Chiri Yukie** 知里幸恵 (1903–1922) wrote down thirteen *kamuy yukar* (カムイユカㇻ, "songs of the gods") in Ainu, in Latin letters, with her own Japanese translation: the ***Ainu shin'yōshū*** 『アイヌ神謡集』 ("Collection of Ainu Songs of the Gods", 1923), the first book of Ainu oral literature written by an Ainu person. She died in Tokyo in 1922, aged nineteen, just after finishing the proofs. The first song, sung by the Owl God (*kamuy cikap kamuy*), opens with the refrain *Shirokanipe ranran pishkan*, "silver drops fall, fall all around" (銀の滴降る降るまわりに). The book is in the public domain in Japan and is available free from Aozora Bunko (青空文庫); Iwanami Bunko (岩波文庫) published a revised edition in 2023.

## Siberia

The great Siberian epics and shamanic cosmologies — Yakut (Sakha) *olonkho*, Buryat Geser, Evenki and Nenets songs of the world tree and the three worlds — were mostly recorded by Russian and Soviet ethnographers in the 19th and 20th centuries, in editions not yet widely translated. The *olonkho* tradition was recognised by UNESCO in 2005. See the archive's [guide to shamanism scholarship](shamanism-scholarship.html) for the main studies and their critics.

## Southeast Asia

Vietnam's origin myth of the Dragon Lord and the Immortal, with its hundred eggs, is now in the archive in the original Classical Chinese: see [The Dragon Lord and the Immortal](dai-viet-hong-bang.html). Other major sources include the Malay *Sejarah Melayu* (Malay Annals), the Javanese and Balinese versions of the Indian epics, and Philippine epics such as the Ifugao *Hudhud*, proclaimed by UNESCO in 2001.

## Aboriginal Australia: a note on protocols

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander creation knowledge, often called the Dreaming, belongs to particular peoples and Country, and some of it is **secret or sacred**: restricted by gender, initiation or kinship. Australian libraries and researchers follow the **AIATSIS Code of Ethics** (2020) and the ATSILIRN protocols, under which such material is not published without the agreement of the communities concerned. For that reason this archive does not reproduce Aboriginal creation narratives. Readers should look for stories published or authorised by the communities themselves, such as those released through Aboriginal-controlled publishers, land councils and language centres.
