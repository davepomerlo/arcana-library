::: note Notice
The manuscript was produced in the context of the Spanish campaigns to "extirpate idolatry": the priest **Francisco de Ávila**, who persecuted the old religion, collected it and added notes in Spanish. Its author or authors were Andean Christians writing about their ancestors' beliefs, and their own position comes through in phrases such as "what faith they held". It is nevertheless the closest thing we have to an Andean scripture in an Andean language. The spelling is the manuscript's own (for example *ñiscap* for modern *ñisqap*, *quillca* for *qillqa*).
:::

## The preface

::: parallel 1
Runa yndio ñiscap Machoncuna ñaupa pacha quillcacta yachanman carca chayca hinantin causascancunapas manam canancamapas chincaycuc hinacho canman
---
If the ancestors of the people called Indians had known writing in ancient times, then all the things they lived through would not be fading away, as they are, even now.
:::

::: parallel 2
himanam viracochappas sinchi cascanpas canancama ricurin hinatacmi canman
---
Rather, just as the might of the Spaniards (the *viracochas*) is still visible today, so theirs would be too.
:::

::: parallel 3
chay hina captinpas canancama mana quillcasca captinpas caypim churani cay huc yayayuc guarocheri ñiscap machoncunap causascanta yma ffeenioccha carcan ymayñah canancamapas causan chay chaycunacta
---
Since that is how things are, and since nothing has been written down until now, I set down here the lives of the ancestors of the people of Huarochirí, who all descend from one forefather: what faith they held, how they live even now, all these things.
:::

::: parallel 4
chayri sapa llactanpim quillcasca canca hima hina causascampas pacariscanmanta
---
And village by village it will be written: how they lived from the time of their dawning.
:::

## What the book contains

The 31 chapters (and two supplements) begin in the deep past. **Chapter 1** tells of the earliest huacas, **Yanañamca** and **Tutañamca**, who were overcome by **Huallallo Carhuincho**, who in turn was defeated and driven out by **Pariacaca**, the snow-mountain god at the centre of the book. **Chapter 2** tells of **Cuniraya Viracocha**, a creator in the guise of a ragged beggar. **Chapter 3** tells of a flood survived by a man warned by a llama, on the peak of Huillcacoto. **Chapter 4** tells of the "death of the sun": five days of darkness when the mortars and grinding stones rose up against their owners.

::: note Words worth knowing
- ***huaca*** (*waka*): any sacred being or place, a mountain, spring, stone or ancestor's body. The huacas of this book fight, marry, travel and turn to stone.
- ***pacarimuy***, "to dawn": in the Andes, peoples and lineages did not come from a single creation but "dawned", each emerging from its own lake, cave or mountain. *Pacarisca*, "dawning", is both origin and origin-place.
- ***viracocha***: the name of a creator god, also used for the Spaniards when they arrived. The writer uses it here simply to mean "Spaniards".
- ***yayayuc***: "having a father", that is, descended from one founding ancestor.
:::
