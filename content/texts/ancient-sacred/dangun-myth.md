::: note Notice
This is a **founding myth** rather than a creation of the world: it explains how the Korean people and state began. It was written down by a Buddhist monk more than three thousand years after the date it gives for Dangun (traditionally 2333 BCE), and he quotes an earlier "Old Record" (古記) that no longer exists. The 1512 reprint used here writes some characters in variant or taboo-avoiding forms; we keep them as printed. Dangun is written 壇君 ("altar lord") in this text and 檀君 ("birch lord") in later sources. The myth has also become a symbol of Korean national identity, and some modern books about it (such as the *Hwandan gogi*, published 1911) are regarded by historians as modern forgeries.
:::

## The son of heaven descends

::: parallel 1
古記云昔有桓囯謂帝釋也庻子桓雄數意天下貪求人世父知子意下視三危太伯可以弘益人間乃授天符印三箇遣徃理之
---
The Old Record says: Long ago there was Hwanin (Hwan'guk) — that is, Indra, Lord of the Gods — whose secondary son Hwanung often set his mind on the world below and longed for the world of humans. His father knew his son's wish. He looked down on Samwi and Taebaek and saw that there he could greatly benefit humankind. So he gave him three heavenly seals and sent him down to rule.
:::

::: parallel 2
雄率徒三千降於太伯山頂即太伯今妙香山神壇樹下謂之神市是謂桓雄天王也將風伯雨師雲師而主糓主命主病主刑主善惡凡主人間三百六十餘事在世理化
---
Hwanung led three thousand followers down to the summit of Mount Taebaek (now Myohyang-san), beneath the Sacred Altar Tree. This was called the City of the Gods, and he was called Heavenly King Hwanung. With the Lord of Wind, the Master of Rain and the Master of Clouds, he took charge of grain, life, sickness, punishment, and good and evil: in all, more than three hundred and sixty human affairs. He lived in the world, ruling and transforming it.
:::

## The bear and the tiger

::: parallel 3
時有一熊一虎同穴而居常祈于神雄願化爲人時神遺靈艾一炷蒜二十枚曰爾輩食之不見日光百日便得人形
---
At that time a bear and a tiger lived in the same cave, and they prayed constantly to the divine Hwanung, wishing to be changed into human beings. The god gave them a bundle of sacred mugwort and twenty cloves of garlic, saying: "Eat these, and do not see the sunlight for a hundred days, and you will gain human form."
:::

::: parallel 4
熊虎得而食之忌三七日熊得女身虎不能忌而不得人身熊女者無與爲婚故每於壇樹下呪願有孕雄乃假化而婚之孕生子號曰壇君王儉
---
The bear and the tiger took them and ate. After keeping the taboo for three times seven days, the bear gained the body of a woman. The tiger could not keep the taboo and did not gain a human body. The bear-woman had no one to marry, so every day beneath the altar tree she prayed for a child. Hwanung then took human form for a time and married her. She conceived and bore a son, who was called Dangun Wanggeom.
:::

The text goes on to say that Dangun founded his capital at Pyongyang, called his kingdom **Joseon**, ruled for 1,500 years, and finally became a mountain god at Asadal, living to the age of 1,908.

::: note Words worth knowing
- **桓因 / 桓囯 Hwanin**: the lord of heaven. Iryeon's note identifies him with **帝釋** (Jeseok, the Buddhist name of the Vedic god Indra, Śakra), a Buddhist reading of an older Korean sky god.
- **弘益人間 *hongik ingan***: "to broadly benefit humankind". Since 1949 it has been written into South Korea's education law as the national ideal of education.
- **神壇樹 *sindansu***: the "sacred altar tree", a world tree linking heaven and earth, as in Siberian and Korean shamanic traditions, where shrines are still set at the foot of sacred trees.
- **三七日**: "three-sevens of days", twenty-one days, the period of seclusion still observed in Korea after a birth.
- **艾 / 蒜**: mugwort (*ssuk*) and garlic, both used in Korean medicine and ritual purification.
:::
