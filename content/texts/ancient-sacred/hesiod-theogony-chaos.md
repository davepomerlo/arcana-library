::: note About this text
Hesiod's *Theogony*, "the birth of the gods", is the oldest Greek account of how the world and the gods came to be. After a long hymn to the Muses, who appeared to him as he herded sheep on Mount Helicon, the poet turns to the beginning. These lines were endlessly discussed in antiquity: Aristotle cited them, and the Stoics and Neoplatonists read philosophy into them.

The first word of the cosmos is *Chaos*. It does not mean "disorder", a later sense, but a gap or chasm: the verb *chainō* means "to gape". Whether Earth, Tartarus and Eros come out of Chaos or simply come next is a famous question; the Greek allows both.
:::

::: parallel 116–122
ἤτοι μὲν πρώτιστα Χάος γένετ᾽· αὐτὰρ ἔπειτα
Γαῖ᾽ εὐρύστερνος, πάντων ἕδος ἀσφαλὲς αἰεὶ
ἀθανάτων οἳ ἔχουσι κάρη νιφόεντος Ὀλύμπου,
[Τάρταρά τ᾽ ἠερόεντα μυχῷ χθονὸς εὐρυοδείης,]
ἠδ᾽ Ἔρος, ὃς κάλλιστος ἐν ἀθανάτοισι θεοῖσι,
λυσιμελής, πάντων τε θεῶν πάντων τ᾽ ἀνθρώπων
δάμναται ἐν στήθεσσι νόον καὶ ἐπίφρονα βουλήν.
---
First of all Chaos came to be; and then
broad-breasted Earth, the ever-secure seat of all
the immortals who hold the peaks of snowy Olympus,
[and misty Tartarus in a recess of the wide-wayed earth,]
and Eros, who is the most beautiful among the immortal gods,
the limb-loosener, who overpowers in the breasts of all gods and all humans
their mind and their prudent counsel.
:::

::: parallel 123–125
ἐκ Χάεος δ᾽ Ἔρεβός τε μέλαινά τε Νὺξ ἐγένοντο·
Νυκτὸς δ᾽ αὖτ᾽ Αἰθήρ τε καὶ Ἡμέρη ἐξεγένοντο,
οὓς τέκε κυσαμένη Ἐρέβει φιλότητι μιγεῖσα.
---
From Chaos came Erebus and black Night;
and from Night in turn came Aether and Day,
whom she conceived and bore after mingling in love with Erebus.
:::

::: note What the scholars say
- **Line 118** is bracketed in West's edition because some ancient readers did not have it and it fits the syntax awkwardly; others defend it.
- **Eros.** Fabienne Blaise (Lille, in French) notes the puzzle that Eros, one of the four first principles, has no children and almost vanishes from the poem. She links him to the outward impulse that drives the births that follow from line 123 on.
- **Chaos.** Guilherme Gontijo Flores (Paraná, in Portuguese) reviews the "rationalist" readings of Chaos as space or air and argues instead for a poetic figure of mystery. Diego Barrios Andrade (Javeriana, in Spanish, 2025) argues the opposite case: that Hesiod could not think past a material beginning, and Chaos is a yawning gap between earth and sky.
- **Night and Day.** Night gives birth to her own opposite, Day: a pattern of opposites born from one another that the early philosophers, Heraclitus above all, took further.
:::
