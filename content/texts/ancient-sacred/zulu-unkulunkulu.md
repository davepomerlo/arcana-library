::: note Notice: who recorded this, and how
These are not ancient texts but oral accounts, recorded about 150 years ago by **Henry Callaway**, an Anglican missionary and doctor in Natal, from Zulu speakers whom he often names. Callaway printed their own words in Zulu beside his English, which makes the book unusually valuable, but he asked the questions, chose what to print, and was looking for traces of a belief in a creator God. Modern scholars (Irving Hexham, Jennifer Weir, David Chidester) disagree about how far "Unkulunkulu" was a creator before missionaries arrived, and how far the idea was shaped by the questioning itself. The spelling is Callaway's 1860s orthography: for example, *kq* stands for the click written *q* today (*wokukqala* = modern *wokuqala*), and *thl* for modern *hl* (*uthlanga* = *uhlanga*).
:::

::: note About the tradition
**Unkulunkulu** means "the old-old one", the great-great-ancestor. In the accounts Callaway collected he is above all the **first man**, who "broke off" (*dabuka*) from **uthlanga**, a reed that sends out shoots, or from a bed of reeds (*umhlanga*), and who then broke off people, cattle and everything else. Other names associated with him are **Umvelinqangi**, "the one who appeared first". He is remembered but no longer prayed to: people pray to the ancestors (*amatongo*) instead.

Several narrators also told the story of the **origin of death**: Unkulunkulu sent the chameleon to tell people "Let not men die", but it dawdled on the way; then he sent the lizard with the message "Let men die", and the lizard arrived first. Similar "failed message" stories are found across much of Africa.
:::

## No longer known

::: parallel 1
Unkulunkulu a s' aziwa. Yena umuntu wokukqala; wa dabuka ekukqaleni.
---
Unkulunkulu is no longer known. He was the first man; he broke off in the beginning.
:::

## Out of the reeds

::: parallel 2
… a ba m bonanga ngamehlo; b' ezwa ngokutshiwo ukuti Unkulunkulu wa be kona. Wa vela lapa abantu ba dabuka kona ohlangeni.
---
… they never saw him with their eyes; they heard it said that Unkulunkulu existed. He came out where people broke off, from the reed.
:::

::: parallel 3
Kwa dabuka abantu, be datshulwa Unkulunkulu.
---
People broke off, broken off by Unkulunkulu.
:::

::: note Words worth knowing
- ***dabuka***: "to break off, to split off, to originate". Callaway noted that the same word is used of a new shoot breaking off from a plant, or of a clan "breaking off" from its founder. Creation is imagined as division, not as making from nothing.
- ***uthlanga*** (modern *uhlanga*): a reed that throws out shoots; by extension the origin or stock of a people. ***ohlangeni*** means "at/from the reed". Callaway records the variant *wa dabuka eluhlangeni*, "he broke off from uthlanga", and *wa dabuka emhlabeni*, "he broke off from the earth".
- ***a s' aziwa***: "he is no longer known". Callaway explains that the same phrase is used of the forgotten founder of a family line.
:::
